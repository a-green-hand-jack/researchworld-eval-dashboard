Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) vary in their abilities on a range of tasks. Initiatives such as the Open LLM Leaderboard aim to quantify these differences with several large benchmarks (sets of test items to which an LLM can respond either correctly or incorrectly).
However, high correlations within and between benchmark scores suggest that (1) there exists a small set of common underlying abilities that these benchmarks measure, and (2) items tap into redundant information and the benchmarks may thus be considerably compressed.
We use data from n>5000n>5000 LLMs to identify the most informative items of six benchmarks, ARC, GSM8K, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA and WinoGrande (with d=28,632d=28,632 items in total). From them we distill a sparse benchmark, metabench, that has less than 3%3\% of the original size of all six benchmarks combined. This new sparse benchmark goes beyond point scores by yielding estimators of the underlying benchmark-specific abilities.
We show that these estimators (1) can be used to reconstruct each original individual benchmark score with, on average, 1.24%1.24\% root mean square error (RMSE), (2) reconstruct the original total score with 0.58%0.58\% RMSE, and (3) have a single underlying common factor whose Spearman correlation with the total score is r=0.94r=0.94.

1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) represent a significant leap forward in our quest to emulate human-like understanding and generation of language (Brown et al., 2020). These sophisticated algorithms are designed to process, interpret, and produce language with unprecedented accuracy and fluency (Bubeck et al., 2023). By training on vast datasets encompassing a myriad of linguistic patterns, LLMs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in tasks ranging from simple text completion (Radford et al., 2019) to complex problem-solving (Chen et al., 2021a) and creative writing (Elkins & Chun, 2020). Their potential to transform various fields—education (Kasneci et al., 2023), customer service (Pandya & Holia, 2023), content creation (Zhang et al., 2023), and beyond—has captured the imagination of researchers and practitioners alike (Bommasani et al., 2021; Bubeck et al., 2023). However, as these models grow in complexity, so does the challenge of effectively evaluating their diverse abilities (Burnell et al., 2023; Ke et al., 2023).

Traditionally, research has relied on extensive benchmarks—vast arrays of tasks—to assess the abilities of LLMs (Srivastava et al., 2023; Raji et al., 2021). However, these benchmarks often measure overlapping skills, leading to redundancy and inefficiency. This paper introduces metabench, a sparse benchmark engineered to distill the essence of six prominent benchmarks—ARC (Clark et al., 2018), GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021), TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022), and WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2020)—into a more concise and informative benchmark. By analyzing data from over 50005000 LLMs, we identified the most revealing items within these benchmarks, compressing them to less than 3% of their combined size. We also provide adaptive testing simulations that suggest that the number of items administered to LLMs can be lowered even further. This volume reduction greatly enhances evaluation efficiency, while also maintaining the original benchmarks’ ability to measure underlying abilities.

The metabench framework leverages psychometric techniques (Hambleton & Swaminathan, 2013; Wang et al., 2023a) to estimate the latent abilities captured by these benchmarks (Burnell et al., 2023).
These estimates can reconstruct individual and total benchmark scores with minimal error, and reveal a single underlying factor that captures most of their variability.
By refining how we measure the abilities of LLMs, metabench offers a new lens through which we can understand and improve them.
Practitioners can run their own models on this efficient and highly informative benchmark, obtain approximated scores, and compute an overall estimate of their models’ general ability.
We additionally offer a second, disjoint version of metabench for users that intend to evaluate an LLM multiple times.
Our work underscores the potential for more streamlined and psychologically meaningful evaluation methods (Coda-Forno et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2023).
It is aimed for the open-source community and independent machine learning researchers with limited resources. All reported analyses and and results can be viewed on www.github.com/adkipnis/metabench and our benchmark itself is available on www.huggingface.co/datasets/HCAI/metabench.

1.1 Related Work

Recent years have seen significant advancements in the development and evaluation of LLMs, resulting in an increased need for efficient benchmarking methods due to the expense of running and querying them. Traditional benchmarks, such as those used in the Open LLM Leaderboard (Beeching et al., 2023), encompass a wide range of tasks. In the current work, we focus on six different benchmarks:
ARC (AI2 Reasoning Challenge, Clark et al., 2018) was designed to test the reasoning abilities of AI through a series of challenging science questions. Similarly, GSM8K (Grade School Math, Cobbe et al., 2021) evaluates the mathematical reasoning capabilities of LLMs by training verifiers on complex word problems. HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019) tests commonsense reasoning by asking models to choose the most plausible continuation of a story from several options. MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding, Hendrycks et al., 2021) assesses the breadth of expert knowledge and reasoning skills across various disciplines, presenting a comprehensive challenge for LLMs. TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022) aims to measure the tendency of language models to generate truthful responses, tackling the issue of misinformation and model-generated falsehoods. Finally, WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2020) tests models on pronoun resolution, a task that requires understanding context and commonsense reasoning. We use these benchmarks to distill an informative, sparse set of items to form metabench.

Psychometric methods including Item Response Theory (IRT) are increasingly used in the evaluation of machine learning models (Martínez-Plumed et al., 2016; Martínez-Plumed et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023a). Zhuang and colleagues (Zhuang et al., 2023) propose using IRT for adaptive testing of LLMs but only used this approach to distinguish between a small set of models and restricted domains and compared models to human responses. Burden and colleagues (Burden et al., 2023) use Bayesian probabilistic programming to infer agents’ capabilities from diverse task performances, building computational models to link task features to system capabilities. However, these methods are applied to reinforcement learning agents and not to LLMs. There also have been several attempts to create completely new questionnaires to evaluate LLMs using psychometric tools (Jiang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023b).

Redundancy in benchmark items has been established, analyzed and combated by several recent works (Rodriguez et al., 2021; Vania et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023c; Ye et al., 2023; Hewitt et al., 2024).
Li et al. (2024) introduce the BenchBuilder pipeline, which filters existing benchmarks to preserve high-quality items. It is used to create Arena-Auto-Hard, a 500-item benchmark which can robustly recover the LLM rankings on both the source benchmark (Chatbot Arena, Chiang et al., 2024) and two other large benchmarks (MT-Bench, Zheng et al., 2023; AlpacaEval 2.0 LC, Taori et al., 2023). BenchBuilder uses LLM annotators to rate benchmark items according to seven criteria to filter out uninformative items. An LLM judge then evaluates whether an LLM subject correctly completes the task specified by the item. Our approach filters prompts in a data-driven manner by first sub-sampling benchmark items and then characterizing item information using IRT. We only use the distribution of responses to benchmarks across a population of LLMs for item filtering, rather than using a priori qualities and LLM annotators which could introduce bias. Similarly to BenchBuilder, our approach is extendable to new benchmarks, provided that they contain a large number of items and that a population of LLMs have completed them.

Most related to our proposal is recent work by Polo et al. (2024) introducing tinyBenchmarks. The authors advocate for reducing the number of evaluation examples in benchmarks to lower costs while maintaining accurate performance assessments of LLMs. They demonstrate that evaluating LLMs on small curated subsets of benchmarks can reliably estimate the performance on large benchmarks like MMLU and HELM (Liang et al., 2022) within a 2% mean absolute error. However, their proposal differs from our approach on several dimensions:

1. 

Model Fit: Polo and colleagues analyzed only 395395 LLMs with high-dimensional IRT models of up to 1515-dimensional latent traits. This raises strong doubts about the goodness of fit for their IRT models, as the number of estimated parameters is far greater than the number of observations (DeMars, 2010; Svetina et al., 2017; Natesan et al., 2016). In comparison, we use over 50005000 LLMs and select 11-dimensional IRT models based on cross-validation.

2. 

Item Selection: The authors base item selection on clustering of the estimated item parameters, which can lead to a bad coverage of the ability landscape. In comparison, we use an information filtering approach which explicitly aims to cover the entire ability landscape.

3. 

Use of Latent Abilities: We go beyond point scores by estimating a latent ability per benchmark and, importantly, show that they jointly permit better score reconstruction for each benchmark’s score and the grand mean. Furthermore, we analyze their latent structure using Factor Analysis and show that they are largely governed by a single general ability.

4. 

Performance: We halve the score reconstruction error reported in tinyBenchmarks: For MMLU resp. Open LLM Leaderboard we reach a mean absolute error of 0.969%0.969\% resp. 0.437%0.437\%.11

2 Benchmark Distillation

We collect benchmark data for over 50005000 LLMs from the Open LLM Leaderboard, and discard items and models based on simple criteria (Section 2.1), reduce each benchmark to a fixed size using cross-validated subsampling (Section 2.2), fit IRT models to quantify the properties of each remaining item (Section 2.3), and finally, we filter out items based on their information content (Section 2.4). We focus on reconstructing the original scores per benchmark using the reduced sets in a cross-validated manner (Sections 2.3 - 2.4). Using Factor Analysis, we uncover strong overlap in the latent abilities measured by each benchmark, and leverage this synergy by combining all benchmarks for score reconstruction (Section 2.5). We also provide preliminary results in Computerized Adaptive Testing (Appendix C.1). For practitioners interested in repeated evaluation, we have created a disjoint second version of metabench that effectively combats over-adaptation to our benchmark (Section 3, see Appendix B.9 for details).

2.1 Data Collection and Preprocessing

We collected openly available item-wise accuracies from Hugging Face Datasets for the six benchmarks that are part of the Open LLM Leaderboard (Beeching et al., 2023): ARC, GSM8K, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA and WinoGrande.
After excluding flagged and merged models, we obtained data from 13551355 unique users, yielding n=6875n=6875 unique LLMs and over 152152 million responses. Out of all LLMs, 50555055 have been evaluated on all six benchmarks. Whenever possible, we used all available LLMs per benchmark for the analyses.

2.2 Cross-Validated Subsampling

For a set of dd items it becomes quickly impossible to consider all subsets of size kk, as there are (dk){d}\choose{k} unique subsets and any exhaustive search algorithm would have 𝒪⁡(d!)\mathcal{O}(d!) time complexity for fixed kk.
The computational infeasibility of finding 100100 items out of the given 2863228632 motivates the approximate methods we present here. Despite the impossibility of exhaustive search, it is astounding how well the given benchmarks can be reduced in a theory-free way using random sampling: Wang et al. (2023c), Ye et al. (2023) and Polo et al. (2024) showed that random samples of benchmark items can in principle produce smaller benchmarks that retain enough information to reconstruct their original score with negligible error. One basic procedure for this is:

1. 

Uniformly sample kk items and calculate the subtest scores s~j\tilde{s}_{j} for each subject jj.

2. 

Fit some regression model s^j=g⁡(s~j)\hat{s}_{j}=g(\tilde{s}_{j}) on the training set.

3. 

Calculate some summary statistic of εj=s^j−sj\varepsilon_{j}=\hat{s}_{j}-s_{j} on the validation set, e.g. its root mean square error (RMSE).

4. 

Repeat steps (1-3) a large number of times.

5. 

Choose the sample with the smallest validation error and check if the performance is still good on the test set.

For g⁡(⋅)g(\cdot) one can use generalized additive models (GAMs, Hastie & Tibshirani, 1987; Fahrmeir et al., 2013; Wood, 2017), which are a flexible class of regression models utilizing basis functions. They can be seen as the frequentist version of the fully Bayesian framework of Gaussian Process regression (Duvenaud et al., 2011).

We ran the described procedure on each benchmark with varying kk and 10,00010,000 repetitions to gauge how information is spread across items. GAMs were fitted using the mgcv package (Wood, 2017) with default options. Appendix B.2 contains the results for kk between 5050 and 500500 in steps of 5050. As shown in Figure 1B, even for k≤200k\leq 200, test errors have high density on relatively small intervals and all median RMSEs are below 3%3\% - many different (but potentially overlapping samples) are informative. This suggests (1) few items are uninformative, (2) the information they carry is partially redundant, and (3) the useful information saturates swiftly as more items are considered. This notion is further explored in Section 2.4. This result has two practical implications: If we just want medium-sized versions of the six benchmarks, we can simply use cross-validated subsampling. If we want to tap into (or even breach) the boundary of the test error distribution, a more principled approach that models properties of items is required. However, principled item selection can be a follow-up after an initial subsampling sweep, as this preselection still permits reliable score reconstruction. For comparability across item property models, and to increase the ratio between the number of observations and estimated parameters, we subsampled all six benchmarks to 350350 items. This number is a compromise between predictive performance and subject-to-item ratio (see Appendix B.2). We next describe the approach to modeling item properties.

2.3 Item Response Theory

Item response theory (IRT) is a statistical branch of psychometrics used for the design and analysis of standardized tests (Chen et al., 2021b; Cai et al., 2016; Irwing et al., 2018; Hambleton et al., 1991). It aims to model the relationship between a subject’s abilities and the probability of a correct response to an item. This framework is closely related to other latent trait models such as factor analysis (Kim & Mueller, 1978; Koch, 2013; Murphy, 2022), but assumes binary or polytomous responses (Chen & Zhang, 2020; Zanon et al., 2016).

A simple and common IRT model is the 2-parameter logistic (2PL) model with 𝜶i=(δi,𝐚i)\bm{\alpha}_{i}=(\delta_{i},\mathbf{a}_{i}) and f⁡(ϑj|𝜶i)=σ⁡(𝐚i⊤​ϑj−δi)f(\bm{\vartheta}_{j}|\bm{\alpha}_{i})=\sigma(\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\vartheta}_{j}-\delta_{i}). 2.4 Information Filtering

Item parameters have a natural interpretation because of how they affect the link function f⁡(ϑj|𝜶i)f(\vartheta_{j}|\bm{\alpha}_{i}): The difficulty δi\delta_{i} shifts its midpoint and the loading aia_{i} alters its scale. Thus, the difficulty parameter determines the probability of a correct response for a subject jj with ϑj=0\vartheta_{j}=0, and the loading determines how much this probability changes with the subject’s ability. An item with a high loading discriminates well between subjects whose ability is close to the item’s difficulty. Following Reckase (2009), an item is more informative if its response is more useful for differentiating between similar values of a subject’s estimated latent ability, ϑ^\hat{\vartheta}. On a high level, this is a measure of how informative an item is about a test taker’s ability conditioned on the ability, and opens up a more principled approach to item selection. For each benchmark we use the following procedure to pick the most predictive items:

1. 

Estimate the item parameters using an IRT model μ∈{2PL, 3PL, 4PL}\mu\in\{\text{2PL, 3PL, 4PL}\}.

2. 

Estimate the ability using a method ν∈{MAP, EAPsum}\nu\in\{\text{MAP, EAPsum}\}.

3. 

Use the item parameters to calculate Fisher information functions for all items.

4. 

Partition the the support of ϑ\vartheta into qq quantiles.

5. 

In each quantile, select the item with the highest information (above a threshold τ\tau).

The same item may have the highest Fisher information in multiple quantiles - to avoid selecting an item multiple times in the same sample, we remove it from the search space after selection.

2.5 Going Meta

Although the previous section indicates that information filtering is in general effective, we can further improve score reconstruction if we do not limit ourselves to using information specific to each benchmark. Benchmark scores are highly correlated (going from r=0.571r=0.571 to r=0.956r=0.956, see Appendix D.2), so each benchmark also carries some information about the others. Thus, if an LLM is run on several reduced benchmarks, the resulting scores may mutually fill in each other’s blanks. Simulations show that this is particularly the case for tests with moderate overlap between the required abilities (see Appendix B.6). We exploit synergy by jointly using information from all six benchmarks as GAM predictors. This joint model is described in more detail in Appendix B.5. As always, IRT models, latent abilities and GAMs were estimated on the training set and the RMSEs reflect the score prediction accuracy on the test set. Figure 1 and the right side of Table 2 show the results: The mean RMSE has shrunk to 1.24%1.24\% and is well below 1.6%1.6\% in all cases. All models surpass the best random subsample by a substantial margin. This synergy effect underlines the advantage of running all components of metabench.

We then applied this method to predict the grand average score: The mean across all normalized scores for the six benchmarks constituting the Open LLM Leaderboad. Random subsets are sampled from each benchmark using its corresponding dd (e.g. 9393 items from HellaSwag), totalling 858858 items. Figure 1C and the bottom row of Table 2 show the score reconstruction results. We were able to reconstruct the grand average with a Spearman correlation of r=0.998r=0.998 and an RMSE of 0.582%0.582\%, which surpassed all random subsamples by a substantial margin. Appendix B.11 shows that these results are stable over random seeds.

Finally, we investigated the relationship between all latent abilities by using factor analysis (Kim & Mueller, 1978; Koch, 2013; Murphy, 2022).
In a nutshell, factor analysis of a set of continuous variables attempts a low-rank approximation of their covariance matrix. As its latent factors have the same interpretation as the latent abilities in IRT, it is commonly applied to analyze the relationship between multiple tests (Irwing et al., 2018). See Appendix D.1 for a more detailed introduction. We applied factor analysis to the estimated latent abilities using the psych package (William Revelle, 2023) (for details, see Appendix D.2). We find that a single factor is sufficient to capture 79.3%79.3\% of the total variability across latent abilities. This meta-ability has a Pearson correlation of r=0.938r=0.938 with the original grand average score. Its loadings range from 0.7710.771 (GSM8K) to 0.9660.966 (WinoGrande): Separately, all six latent abilities contribute a comparable amount to the meta-ability, but the added unique information decreases with each. This result is in line with the synergy effect as described above. It consolidates the utility of metabench for efficiently measuring the abilities of LLMs.

3.1 Score Reconstruction

Our benchmark is hosted as a dataset on HuggingFace and is integrated in EleutherAI’s lm-evaluation-harness (Gao et al., 2024), a unified framework for evaluating language models on a range of tasks. We provide two disjoint versions of metabench containing distinct items for all six benchmarks (see Appendix B.9). This way, metabench can be used for repeated evaluation. 3.2 Memorization Mitigation

The described four variants of metabench aim to mitigate the problem of contamination and benchmark memorization. A model trained on the standard version of the benchmark will not perform appreciably better on the other versions: We evaluated three LLMs on each version and then fine-tuned their low-rank adapters on one version with fixed choice ordering. Fine-tuning was done using unsloth on 4-bit quantized instruct models. We then re-evaluated each LLM on all four versions of metabench. 4 Conclusion

The findings presented in this study underscore the viability and advantages of using a sparse benchmark, metabench, to measure the abilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). We successfully distill six prominent benchmarks — ARC, GSM8K, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA, and WinoGrande — into a much smaller but equally informative set of items. The use of latent abilities enables accurate reconstruction of individual and total benchmark scores, as well as the original ranking: metabench retains the evaluative power of the original, larger benchmarks while by reducing redundancy. This successful distillation of six large benchmarks to less than 3%3\% of the original volume highlights the potential for more streamlined, cost-effective evaluation methods. Efficiency is particularly valuable for researchers and developers who need to frequently evaluate and compare models during prototyping — without the computational and time constraints associated with larger benchmarks. Our methodology can be a stepping stone for future work on benchmark distillation. Finally, the identification of a single common factor underlying all benchmark scores suggests that a general ability for reasoning and knowledge can be effectively measured through a consolidated, sparse benchmark. This finding supports the hypothesis that LLM performance across various tasks is influenced by a core set of underlying competencies. By distilling these competencies into a concise benchmark, metabench offers a more focused and insightful evaluation tool.

4.1 Limitations

Our work has two main limitations. First,
static benchmarks run the risk of being memorized (Magar & Schwartz, 2022), and the smaller the benchmark, the higher the risk. Of course, this is a general problem with static benchmarks, and ours is not totally immune to it in its current form, although the mitigation strategies discussed in Section 3.2 go some way to attenuating the performance boost garnered from training directly on a component of metabench. We see three further potential ways to mitigate memorization. (1) One could let another language model generate semantically equivalent but differently worded test items, reducing the probability of exact item memorization (Moros Daval, 2023). (2) One could test whether an LLM completes the wording of a question from the test set when prompted with the beginning of the question. (3) One could use computerized adaptive testing to select test items from a much larger pool of items, reducing the likelihood of memorization across the full set. Each of these extensions should be included in future attempts of benchmark distillation.
Importantly, however, there is no way for us to test if the data we used for IRT models has been contaminated (without pulling each LLM and testing it individually). If we assume that the rate of memorization does not drastically change between the models used for training and models that will later be tested on metabench, our results suggest that data contamination is not a major issue for score reconstruction.

The second limitation concerns the conditional independence of the LLMs we used. Most users who submitted results to the OpenLLM Leaderboard contributed several models, potentially violating the IRT model assumption that the response patterns of different subjects are independent (conditional on the item parameters). This may result in biased parameter estimates and therefore distorted item information functions. Indeed, the population of LLMs presumably has a different dependence structure than than that of human test takers: Many LLMs are fine-tuned versions of others or at least share the same base structure and are trained on overlapping data. In the extreme case, treating them as subjects for IRT models is akin to analyzing a test on several batches of clones. On the other hand, the same can be said about human subjects - despite sharing many fundamental properties of the human mind and brain, and experiencing overlapping school curricula, human subjects measurably differ in their knowledge, problem solving abilities, linguistic skills and memory (Tyler, 1947; Eysenck, 2014).
Typically, clustering in human data is treated by including random effects to (generalized) regression models (Fahrmeir et al., 2013). This is not possible for IRT models, as these random effects would become indistinguishable from latent abilities without introducing further constraints. However, the empirical distributions of point scores remain largely unchanged if we only include the latest LLM per leaderboard user (see Appendix B.1). This suggests, that the dependence structure is not strongly affected by model clustering over users. As the ratio between subjects and items is already low by IRT standards, substantial discarding of subjects without also reducing the item sets is prohibitive for IRT modeling. Future work with more data should analyze to what extent similarities in fine-tuning and architecture deflate predictive performance for scores and (more importantly) change the ranking outcomes. Finally, it is likely that new LLMs tested on these benchmarks are themselves not far out of distribution with respect to the training set, making it very likely that the non-parametric test error bounds are still accurate.

4.2 Future Work

Alongside remedying the limitations of this study, we see several avenues for future work. First, metabench can be validated on LLMs released after our analyses were completed. Second, our methodology can be applied to other domains beyond natural language processing, helping to develop sparse benchmarks for computer vision, reinforcement learning, and speech recognition. Third, the development of adaptive testing algorithms and interfaces could further reduce the number of items required for accurate LLM evaluation. By continuously refining the selection process based on real-time performance data, these algorithms could enhance the efficiency and precision of LLM assessments. Finally, the core set of cognitive abilities in LLMs remains largely unknown. Future research should closely investigate its structure, as well as uncover the properties of model architecture and training data that generate certain ability profiles. This would constitute a basis for more goal-directed and theoretically grounded benchmarks, driving progress towards more capable LLMs whose behavior we can predict and interpret.

4.3 Ethics Statement

We use publicly available benchmark data from LLMs on huggingface.co. Our study does not include human subjects and does not reveal the correct answers to any of the benchmarks. In particular, each of the studied benchmarks is standard and broadly accepted by the LLM community. We anticipate no harmful effects from our findings. On the contrary, this project aims to contribute towards more resource fairness and energy efficiency in LLM research.
