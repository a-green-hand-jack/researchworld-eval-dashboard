## F1 — leverage predicts the variance when the model is right (grids e1b, e1) → C1 supported in the iid slice; the network sandwich is not a usable interval at high d/n
REG-L / REG-O on a linear response, sigma_idio = 1, ER/SBM/BA/WS, d in {5,10,20,40}, p in {0.2,0.5}, R = 300:
sd(tau_hat) / sqrt(sigma^2 L / n) lies in [0.94, 1.09] in all 32 cells (L = corner leverage averaged over replicate graphs).
The iid OLS interval covers 0.91–0.97; the pre-registered network sandwich (closed 2-hop dependence pattern) **under-covers as d grows even though
the model is exactly right**: coverage 0.55 (ER, d=40, p=0.5), 0.58 (BA), 0.63 (SBM), 0.79 (WS) at n = 2000; its SE is 0.68–0.9× the true sd.
The count-feature variant REG-Lc, applied to a fraction-exposure response, is biased (coverage 0–0.9; ER d=5, p=0.5 coverage 0.00).

**F1b — scaling in n, d, p, sigma (grid e1: ER, REG-L, linear response, correct model, n ∈ {500…8000}, d ∈ {5…80}, p ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 0.5}, sigma ∈ {0.5, 1}, R = 400, 150 cells).**
sd(tau_hat)/sqrt(sigma^2 L/n) has median 1.01 and range 0.90–1.15 over all 150 cells (no trend in n, d, p or sigma; Monte-Carlo noise of an sd at R = 400 is ≈ 3.5%, and the extremes lie at n = 500 and d = 80). The iid OLS interval covers 0.92–0.97 in all 144 cells where it is computed (median 0.95); REG-L's mean bias is 0.0005 in units of the outcome scale.
The pre-registered sandwich is worse the larger d²/n: SE/true-sd median 0.98 for d²/n < 0.05, 0.85 for d²/n ≈ 0.2–0.5, 0.66 for 0.5–1.9, 0.24 for d²/n > 1.9; coverage falls from 0.94 (d = 5) to 0.83 (d = 20), 0.60 (d = 40) and 0.23 (d = 80, median; min 0.005 at n = 500, d = 80).
At n = 8000 it recovers (median coverage 0.93) except d = 80, where the sandwich was **not run** (memory guard n(d+1)² ≤ 3·10⁷; 6 cells, marked NaN, not imputed). The iid SE is the right one here because the model is correct and errors are independent; the sandwich's failure is a many-dependent-pairs (small-sample) effect, and e9/e10 test the alternatives.
Condition: this is the correctly specified, homoskedastic slice; it says nothing about leverage when the model is wrong (that is C2/C4).

## F2 — the bias floor equals the exact projection bias and does not shrink with n (grid e2b) → C2 supported
ER, d = 20, p = 0.25, kappa = 1, n from 250 to 8000, R = 200: measured REG-L bias vs exact projection bias (design-only Monte Carlo,
independent seeds): convex −0.47 vs −0.473 at every n (MC SE 0.004–0.03); threshold −0.14…−0.18 vs −0.145…−0.149. sd falls as n^-1/2
(0.37 → 0.058 at sigma_idio = 0 with frozen heterogeneity), so for n ≥ 1000 the error is bias-dominated.
Flexibility does not fix this: REG-Q has 3.7× the sd of REG-L at n = 8000, and for the threshold response its bias is **+1.5** (REG-L: −0.15) — it
extrapolates the wrong way; REG-O (true shape known) is unbiased with ≈1.8× the sd of REG-L (leverage of g^2 feature).

**F2b — bias prediction across the knob grid (grid e2: ER, n = 2000, kappa ∈ {0, .25, .5, 1} × shape {convex, concave, thresh} × p ∈ {.1, .25, .5, .75} × d ∈ {5, 10, 20, 50} × sigma ∈ {0, .5}, 320 cells, R = 200).**
Exact projection bias (computed from the graph and design with its own seeds, independent of the replicates) vs the measured bias of REG-L: correlation 0.9996 over the 320 cells; median |measured − predicted| = 0.006, max 0.033;
z = (measured − predicted)/√(se_meas² + se_pred²): 3.4% of cells have |z| > 2 and none |z| > 3 (nominal 5%, 0.3%) — no sign of a systematic gap.
The same prediction holds for the other fixed-feature regressions up to their larger Monte-Carlo noise (REG-Q correlation 0.977, REG-G 0.987; the largest REG-Q discrepancies are the d = 50, p = 0.1 cells where its sd is ≈ 3.7 and the bias se 0.26, |z| < 3). REG-O (true form) has no bias: median |bias| < 0.01, max 0.18 at the same high-variance corner.
Size and where it lives: |bias| grows with kappa (median 0.11, 0.23, 0.46 at kappa = .25, .5, 1 for convex/concave; thresh 0.08, 0.17, 0.33), with d (median 0.08 at d = 5, 0.22 at d = 50) and with the asymmetry of p: it is ≈ 0 at p = 0.5 for the symmetric convex/concave shapes
(median 0.007) and 0.34 at p = 0.1. **The bias floor is therefore a function of p and shape, not just of kappa**; the thresh shape at p = 0.5 is not symmetric and keeps a bias of ≈ 1.0 at kappa = 1 (see F3).
Consequences for error: for kappa > 0 the squared bias is a median 67% of REG-L's MSE; REG-L beats DM in 98.6% of kappa > 0 cells (DM RMSE ≈ 1.00, bias −gamma), beats REG-Q in 74%, REG-G (fixed features) in 100%; REG-O (oracle form) beats REG-L in 95% — a bound, not a competitor.
Over all 320 cells REG-Q beats REG-L in 75 (median RMSE ratio 1.68; 4–14 at d = 50, p = 0.1, mean 8.2), i.e. extra flexibility without the right form costs variance faster than it removes bias.
Coverage under misspecification: iid interval covers 0.70 (median) and 0.56 (mean) for kappa > 0, down to 0.00; the sandwich 0.44 (median) — neither accounts for bias (this is what H7 / the bias-aware interval in e7 addresses).

## F3 — regression vs HT/Hájek vs DM (grid e3; Bernoulli, n ≈ 2000, sigma = 0.5, d from 2 to 50) → **the outlined crossover (C3/H8) is NOT supported as stated**
Exact design-based RMSE of full-exposure HT (exact variance from the pair formula; validated against 2·10^5 Monte Carlo draws: var 1.185 vs 1.188):
0.28 (d=2, p=.5) → 0.97 (d=5) → 12 (d=10) → 1.8·10^3 (d=20) → 2.6·10^9 (d=50), i.e. exponential in d. REG-L: 0.04–0.16 over the same range in linear cells.
REG-L beats exact HT at every d ≥ 2 in all 10 (mechanism, p) cells (100 (cell,d) pairs); the crossover with unbiased HT sits at d ≈ 1–2.
Against the practical Hájek form (arm-mean fallback when nothing is exposed; it degenerates into DM, RMSE → 1.0, for d ≳ 15–20) REG-L is better in 86 of 100 (cell,d) pairs.
The 14 where Hájek is better (REG-L bias-dominated; differences ≤ 0.03 for convex kappa=1 p=0.2 at d=3–5) are mostly the threshold kappa = 1, p = 0.5 cell, for every d:
REG-L RMSE 0.09 (d=2), 0.38 (d=5), 0.83 (d=10), 1.24 (d=20), 1.61 (d=50) vs Hájek 0.07, 0.19, 0.49, 0.98, 1.00.
A *relaxed* exposure Hájek (delta = 0.25, biased by construction) is far better than REG-L in that cell (0.05 vs 0.17 at d=3; 0.24 vs 1.24 at d=20): the real
competitor in the bias-dominated regime is relaxed exposure, not exact HT. DM RMSE ≈ 1.0 (bias = −γ) in every cell; REG-L beats DM in 96 of 100 pairs and is **worse than DM**
only in thresh kappa = 1, p = 0.5 for d ≥ 15 (1.08–1.61 vs 1.00). REG-Q at p = 0.2 blows up with d (1.5 at d=50 on a linear response vs REG-L 0.19).

## F4 — staggered rollout at matched measurements (grid e8; ER n=2000, d in {10,40}, T+1 = 3, 5, 9 measurements) → C7 partly
Repeating T+1 independent Bernoulli(0.5) experiments and pooling the regression lowers sd (0.071 → 0.026 at d=10) but leaves the bias unchanged
(threshold: +0.83 at every T; 2-hop convex: −0.50). Staggered rollout (corner observed, no-carry-over assumed): STAG-direct is unbiased with sd 0.015 (noise only);
the pooled linear regression on rollout data is nearly unbiased but its bias *grows* with T (threshold d=10: 0.04, 0.13, 0.19 for T = 2, 4, 8)
because interior stages dominate the fit. The corner has to be in the data, not only more data; STAG buys this with its stated extra assumptions.

## F5 — design-only variance: every standard error is about right; coverage fails through bias, not through the SE (grid e10: graph, heterogeneity and noise frozen per cell, only the assignment redrawn; REG-L; 128 cells = 4 graph families × d ∈ {5, 10, 20, 40} × p ∈ {.2, .5} × 4 responses, R = 500)
Ratio of the mean estimated SE to the design sd of tau_hat (median [5%, 95%] over the 128 cells): iid-OLS 0.98 [0.87, 1.06], HC3 0.99 [0.89, 1.06], cluster-robust 0.99 [0.89, 1.05], assignment resampling ("rerand") 0.98 [0.89, 1.04], **pre-registered network sandwich 0.95 [0.74, 1.03]**.
The sandwich is the only one with a visible deficit, concentrated at d = 40 (median ratio 0.78, range 0.70–0.91, all four families); at d ≤ 10 it is 0.98–1.00. (The design sd itself has ≈ 3% Monte-Carlo error at R = 500.)
So when the heterogeneity is frozen, the iid-residual variance is not far from the true design variance in this family (it does not show the 2–9× gap that motivated the cell design); the e1 result (sandwich failure at large d²/n) reappears as d = 40 at n = 2000.
Coverage is governed by bias: responses where REG-L is misspecified (convex/thresh kappa = 1; linear in the 2-hop exposure with w2 = 0.5, where REG-L's 1-hop feature omits half the effect: bias ≈ 0.5) cover 0.50 / 0.06 / 0.00 (median over cells) with every SE, while the correctly specified linear response covers 0.95 (iid, HC2, HC3, resampling) and 0.92 (sandwich, CRVE).
Restricting to the 50 cells whose measured |bias| < 0.5 sd (a post-hoc selection, so indicative only) all eight intervals cover 0.89–0.95. Conclusion for the interval claim (C6): no choice of the variance estimator repairs coverage; only a bias term (H7) or a different estimand can. Condition: Bernoulli design, n = 2000, REG-L only.

## F7 — the collapse (grid e4; REG-L; Bernoulli p ∈ {.1, .25, .5}; ER, SBM, BA, WS at n = 2000 and d ∈ {5, 10, 20, 40, 80}; Stanford3 (n up to 11 586) and ego-Facebook thinned to q ∈ {.05…1} / {.1…1}; 3 responses; R = 200; 261 cells, 87 per response; `scripts/analyze_c4.py`) → C4 holds with the harmonic-mean degree; the mean degree fails on degree-heterogeneous graphs; one of (Z0, Z1) is not enough
Z0 = p/s, Z1 = (1−p)/s with s² = p(1−p)/d for a degree summary d.
(A) Variance, no fitted parameter: sd(tau_hat)·√n/σ_tot against Z0 + Z1 = 1/s on the correctly specified linear response (29 graph settings × 3 p = 87 cells). With d = harmonic mean degree: R² of the identity line 0.97, rank agreement (Spearman) 0.99, median |log ratio| 5.7% (mean log ratio +0.06; the Monte-Carlo error of an sd at R = 200 is ≈ 5%). By family R² is 0.82 (ego-Facebook), 0.85 (Stanford3), 0.98–0.99 (ER, SBM, BA, WS). With the **mean** degree the same identity gives R² 0.61 overall and −5.9 on Stanford3 (observed sd is 0.54× the prediction, since the mean degree overstates the exposure variance), −1.3 on ego-Facebook, but 0.97–0.995 on ER, SBM, WS, BA (BA with mean degree 0.965, harmonic 0.975): the mean degree is only wrong where the degree distribution is heavy-tailed or the graph has very-low-degree nodes. The harmonic mean is the right summary because the pooled exposure variance is p(1−p)·mean(1/d_i).
(B) Bias, leave-one-family-out (cubic polynomial of (log Z1, log Z0) fitted on five families, predicted for the sixth; bias normalised by the outcome scale; threshold response kappa = .5 and concave kappa = 1 with sigma_alpha = .5; the linear response has no bias): held-out R² 0.967 (threshold) and 0.897 (concave) with harmonic Z, 0.770 and 0.750 with mean-degree Z; the worst held-out family is Stanford3 (prediction RMSE 0.056 and 0.080 against sd 0.175 and 0.139 of the normalised bias). Rank agreement 0.98 and 0.90.
Two honest qualifications. (1) Z adds nothing over its inputs: a polynomial in (log d_hm, p) predicts as well (R² 0.966 / 0.897) because (Z0, Z1) is a re-parametrisation of (d, p); the claim carried is "the degree summary that works is the harmonic mean, and the error is a function of two numbers (d_hm, p)", not that Z is a new variable. (2) One coordinate is not enough: Z1 alone (R² 0.27 thresh, 0.62 concave) or Z0 alone (0.48, 0.04) fails across p; the collapse is in the pair, or in Z0 + Z1 for the variance only.
Not tested here: dependence on n (fixed ≈ 2000, except the real graphs), Bernoulli design only (GCR via d_eff is in e5, F6), REG-L only, one threshold and one concave response. Stanford3 at q = 1 has no sandwich SE (memory guard; 9 cells = 3 responses × 3 p).

## F13 — why REG-L on the threshold response at θ = .5 is far better on ego-Facebook and Stanford3 than on the synthetic graphs (review finding M3; exact projection bias, `scripts/analyze_c13.py`, `c13_exposure.csv`, `c13_fit_subset.csv`; Bernoulli p = .5, same graphs as grid e6: ER/SBM/BA/WS n = 2000 d = 20, ego-Facebook q = .5 (n = 3950 after thinning), Stanford3 q = .2 (n = 10 481); design-exact, no replicates) → **a mechanism, tested by an intervention: the step lies inside the data only where units of low degree spread g over [0, 1]**
Mechanism. REG-L fits the slope of the response on g = treated share of neighbours, and OLS gives it the slope where g has mass. At degree ≈ 20 and p = .5, g has sd ≈ .11 around .5 (ER .112, SBM .117, WS .112), exactly where the logistic step (θ = .5, steepness 12) is steepest, so the fitted slope is that local steepness (2.23 on ER, 2.20 SBM, 2.25 WS, 2.06 BA; the response's chord from 0 to 1 has slope 1) and the line extrapolated from g ≈ .5 to the corner g = 1 overshoots the plateau by slope − 1 times the distance: bias +1.23 (ER). On ego-Facebook and Stanford3, 23–24% of units have degree ≤ 5 (43% / 37% ≤ 10), their g ranges over {0, …, 1} and puts both plateaus in the sample: sd(g) over units is .20–.21, the share of units whose g falls at a plateau (g ≤ .2 or ≥ .8, binomial, exact) is .13–.15 against .008–.012 on ER/SBM/WS and .037 on BA, and the fitted slope falls to 1.41 / 1.34, bias +0.41 / +0.34 (normalised .21 / .17).
Agreement with the measurement: the exact value reproduces the measured REG-L bias of grid e6 on all six graphs (exact / measured: ER 1.231 / 1.243 ± .010, SBM 1.195 / 1.181 ± .010, BA 1.056 / 1.053 ± .010, WS 1.247 / 1.266 ± .012, ego-Facebook 0.414 / 0.411 ± .004, Stanford3 0.337 / 0.335 ± .003; synthetic graphs are fresh draws in e6, one draw here). Across the six graphs the exact bias is a strictly decreasing function of the plateau mass (Spearman −1, n = 6: an ordering, not a fit).
Not heterogeneity as such: BA is degree-heterogeneous (cv .95) but has minimum degree 10 (no unit with degree ≤ 5; 16% ≤ 10), plateau mass .037, and its bias (1.06) is 14% below ER's, nowhere near the real graphs'. What matters is the mass of units whose g can reach a plateau.
Intervention (`c13_fit_subset.csv`; the same exact projection with the regression fitted only on units in a degree range, the corner prediction still over all units): fitted on degree ≤ 5 only, ego-Facebook / Stanford3 give bias 0.14 / 0.10 (slope 1.14 / 1.09); on degree 6–11 only, 0.77 / 0.77; on degree ≥ 12 only, 1.26 / 1.29, i.e. the synthetic values (1.15–1.25 at degree ≥ 12 on BA, SBM, ER, WS), and 1.45 / 1.43 on degree ≥ 20.
Conditions: Bernoulli p = .5 (the step sits at the centre of the exposure distribution; at θ = .3 the bias is small everywhere, .07–.21 (not analysed further): ER .193, BA .214, ego-Facebook .102, Stanford3 .068; the real graphs are again the lowest); REG-L only; one steepness (12); thinned real graphs (the degree ≤ 5 shares are those after the thinning q); the intervention is an exact projection with a restricted fit, not a re-randomisation. The conclusion for §5.5(i): the real-graph advantage is conditional on a low-degree mass in the sample and would disappear on a real graph with minimum degree ≥ 10; this follows from the corner-extrapolation account, it is not a separate effect.
