# Exact results behind the algorithms

Let H be the largest rival aggregate, and y_j the target's score after selecting task j. Co-first uses threshold H (up to the stated floating tolerance). Rivals' cardinal aggregates are fixed.

For the arithmetic mean, an intervention succeeds iff sum_j z_j(y_j-x_j) >= nH-sum_j x_j. Since costs are one and gains nonnegative, the shortest prefix of gains sorted in descending order is optimal: exchanging any selected smaller gain for an unselected larger gain never reduces success. The geometric mean has the same argument in log scores after forcing all zero-score tasks to become positive when H>0. These statements would not justify gain-per-cost greedy selection with heterogeneous task costs.

For n=2h-1 and median, at least h target scores must meet H. Let q be the number already meeting H and r the number of below-H tasks whose allowed improved score meets H. The exact optimum is max(0,h-q) if r is at least that deficit, otherwise it is infeasible. Each selected task changes the count by at most one; selecting any required number of eligible tasks attains the lower bound. For unit ceilings with full improvement this gives a universal upper bound h, regardless of the target's distance from rivals. For n=2h the conventional median averages two order statistics; the odd-n formula does not apply. We enumerate all subsets for the six-task benchmark. Selecting h+1 tasks and setting them to one always suffices for co-first, but a smaller number may work depending on the two central order statistics.

For the minimum, every target score below H must be improved to at least H. The exact count is therefore the number below H when all can reach H, otherwise infeasible. This can be as large as n; it can also be much smaller because H itself is the best rival's weakest-task score. Calling the minimum a robust aggregation rule does not by itself imply a high strategic budget.

For Borda, define pairwise points p(a,b)=1[a>b]+0.5*1[a=b]. Its task points are sum_{i != t}p(x_tj,x_ij). Let d_ij=p(y_j,x_ij)-p(x_tj,x_ij) and g_j=sum_{i != t}d_ij. If task j is selected, the target gains g_j and rival i loses d_ij, so the exact winning constraints are sum_j(g_j+d_ij)z_j >= B_i-B_t for every rival. Summing independent gains while freezing rival Borda scores would be wrong. These binary constraints give the exact minimum with MILP optimality certification, or by exhaustive subsets when there are six tasks. Strict Borda improvement requires half a point because Borda scores are half-integral.

For all five rules, improving a selected target coordinate cannot worsen its relative standing, including through rivals' Borda changes. Consequently the minimum budget is nonincreasing in alpha and the ceiling coordinatewise, with infinity for infeasible settings. This is a checkable property and a reason not to compare only the reachable subsets when gain assumptions change: that changes the target population.

No theorem here orders all aggregation rules by strategic robustness. Their different strongest rivals, target gaps and task score profiles determine the empirical comparison. A constructive per-target subset certifies score-space feasibility under the assumed score access; it is not evidence that a training process can realize that subset, and does not bound real training cost.
