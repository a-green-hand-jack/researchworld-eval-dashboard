# Plain-language summary

A leaderboard can change when only a few recorded comparisons are altered. That does not necessarily mean that typical random mistakes will change it, or that the leading participants are meaningfully different to begin with.

We analyzed public model-comparison votes, expert and GPT-4 judgments, and two NBA seasons. We kept the observed pairings fixed and reversed selected win/loss records. The top two participants swapped after one selected Arena record, 46 human MT-Bench judgments, 61 GPT-4 MT-Bench judgments, and four or eight NBA games. The general search demonstrates changes that are possible; it does not usually prove that no smaller set exists. The complete GPT-4 comparison design permits an exact proof of its minimum.

Randomly reversing the same number of records rarely had the same effect. Other choices also mattered: removing ties changed Arena's original top-two order, and in NBA 2015 a third-place team was a cheaper tested challenger than the runner-up. We also compared human and GPT-4 judgments using identical balanced sets of questions and model pairs. The GPT-4 judgments consistently required more reversals in those sampled panels, but this could reflect preferences or bias rather than better judging.

The findings apply to these historical data and this scoring model. They suggest that a stability claim should state what was changed, which ranking distinction was tested, how often the focal participants were compared, and how uncertain the original scores were. No live votes were submitted or sporting events altered.
