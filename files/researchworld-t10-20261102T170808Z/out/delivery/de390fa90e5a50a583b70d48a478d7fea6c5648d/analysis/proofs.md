# Mathematical checks and limits

These are transparent control-variate calculations and a specialization of an existing concentration theorem; no novelty claim is made for them.

## 1. Optimum for deterministic score controls

Assume L consists of n iid pairs (Y,S), U consists of N iid S values from the same marginal distribution, independent of L. Let E[Y^2]<infinity and h(S) be square integrable. For theta(h)=bar(Y-h)_L+bar h_U, E theta(h)=mu and

n Var(theta(h)) = Var(Y)-2 Cov(Y,h)+(1+r)Var(h), r=n/N.

Writing m(S)=E[Y|S], the tower property gives Cov(Y,h)=Cov(m,h). Therefore

n Var(theta(h)) = Var(Y)-Var(m)/(1+r)+(1+r)Var(h-m/(1+r)).

Consequently the best deterministic square-integrable score control is h*=m/(1+r), up to an additive constant, and

Var(theta(h*)) = E Var(Y|S)/n+Var(m)/(n+N).

This optimization is restricted to unbiased mean estimators made from a score control; it does not establish a universal finite-sample CI-length bound or rule out additional assumptions, other covariates, or specialized inference methods. A constant conditional mean yields no population variance reduction from any such score transformation. An affine m makes the unrestricted scalar control optimal in this class. Finite N contributes Var(m)/(n+N) even with perfect conditional-mean knowledge.

## 2. Fixed basis and scalar comparison

Let centered B(S) be a fixed p-dimensional vector of square-integrable functions with positive-definite covariance G. The population regression coefficient gamma solves G gamma=Cov(B,Y), and h_B=B^T gamma/(1+r). If the scalar space generated by centered S is contained in span(B), the projection identity gives

Var(theta(h_scalar))-Var(theta(h_B)) = [Var(Pi_B m)-Var(Pi_scalar m)]/[n(1+r)] >= 0.

Equality holds exactly when the added basis directions have zero partial covariance with Y. This is a population-coefficient statement. The finite-sample learned estimate has additional nuisance-fitting variability, so there is no pointwise or average-width guarantee for the implemented cubic at a given n. The ppi-python implementation clips its scalar coefficient to [0,1]; the unrestricted scalar is included so that basis gains cannot be explained only by this restriction.

For the quadratic Gaussian generator, m=(2S-1)^2 and S~Uniform[0,1]. E m=1/3, Var(m)=4/45, Cov(m,S)=0, noise variance=.15^2. The cubic basis contains m modulo constants. The oracle width ratio versus the scalar optimum is

sqrt((.15^2+(4/45)*r/(1+r))/(.15^2+4/45)).

It equals approximately .524 at r=.1 and .775 at r=1. Exact values for all analytically tractable generators are produced by code/theory.py in results/theory.csv; these are theoretical predictions, not measured intervals.

## 3. Cross-fitting asymptotics

Assume fixed p and fixed K=2, each fold has a positive limiting fraction of n, G is nonsingular, E[Y^2]+E[||B||^2]<infinity, n/N tends to a positive finite r, and the limiting oracle variance is positive. Centered least squares on either training fold converges to gamma. Let b*=gamma/(1+n/N) and b_{-k} be the fitted coefficient. The difference between the cross-fitted estimator and theta(B^T b*) is

sum_k w_k (bar B_U-bar B_{L_k})^T (b_{-k}-b*).

Each difference in feature means is O_p(n^(-1/2)+N^(-1/2)); each coefficient error is o_p(1). There are only two folds, so this remainder is o_p(n^(-1/2)). Dependence from sharing U across folds does not change this finite-sum argument. The CLT for the two independent oracle samples gives the normal limit. The empirical variances of out-of-fold Y-B^T b_{-k} and averaged-fold B_U controls converge to the two oracle variances, by consistency of coefficients and sample second moments. Slutsky's theorem gives asymptotic 1-alpha coverage of the implemented Wald interval.

No finite-sample guarantee follows from this proof. Near-singular design covariance, too many basis terms for available labels, rare outcomes and heavy tails can make the approximation poor. The experiment deliberately retains such failures. Dimension 12 is a diagnostic at a fixed p; the argument does not justify p growing arbitrarily with n.

## 4. Finite-sample bounded-data guarantee

Assume independent iid draws as above and Y,S in [0,1]. Maurer and Pontil (2009), Theorem 4 (directly read at arXiv:0907.3740), bounds one tail for observations in [0,1] with radius sqrt(2 v log(2/delta)/k)+7 log(2/delta)/(3(k-1)), where v is unbiased sample variance. Rescale from a known interval of length R and union-bound the two tails, obtaining

b(Z,R,delta)=sqrt(2 v log(4/delta)/k)+7 R log(4/delta)/(3(k-1)).

After reserving q=floor(n/4) training pairs (at least 10), condition on those pairs. Every fitted h used in the EB methods is then a deterministic function, with known range [0,c]. Evaluation residuals are iid in [-c,1] of range length 1+c; U controls are iid in [0,c] of length c. Use b on each mean with error alpha/2 and add the radii. By a union bound, the sum of means covers mu with probability at least 1-alpha, conditional on training and hence unconditionally. No assumption of correct calibration, homoskedasticity, Gaussianity, or regression consistency is required. Intersecting the interval with the known parameter space [0,1] preserves coverage.

`eb_label` needs no training and uses all n. `eb_ppi_full` likewise uses all n because h=S is fixed. The old common-split `eb_ppi` is a diagnostic, never the strongest unit-PPI comparison. `eb_scalar`/`eb_cubic` use only the original n labels including training, never extra labels. Empty or poorly fitted training regression does not break coverage because its clipped function remains bounded. The finite-sample gain claim is restricted to the specified EB comparators; exact binomial Clopper-Pearson is additionally reported wherever outcomes are binary.

## 5. What measured coverage means

A Wilson Monte Carlo interval that contains .95 establishes compatibility with the target, not proof of exact coverage. A Holm-corrected one-sided test diagnoses statistically detectable undercoverage, not a universal certificate for all distributions. Exact EB and CP guarantees come from their stated sampling assumptions and theorems, independently of observed hit counts. No Wald interval is described as satisfying a finite-sample 95% guarantee.
