2 Background for Split Conformal Inference Let (U_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,n+1} be (n+1) exchangeable vectors of random variables, with U_{i}=(X_{i},Y_{i})\in\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y} .
This means that the joint distribution of (U_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,n+1} is invariant by any permutation of the symmetric group of order (n+1) . For example, an i.i.d. sample satisfies this assumption. In our framework we assume that (U_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,n} , called the calibration set (or calibration sample), has been observed and that we get X_{n+1} . We assume further that we have at hand a predictor \widehat{f}(X_{n+1}) of Y_{n+1} . In this paper, for the sake of simplicity we assume that this predictor is deterministic. For example, it had been built using a sample independent of (U_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,n+1} and so we are working conditionally to the training sample (this is the main assumption of split CI, in contrast with full CI where \widehat{f} is retrained for each new sample point).
The aim is to provide a prediction set for Y_{n+1} .
More precisely, as discussed in the Introduction, our objective is to construct a conservatively valid prediction set for Y_{n+1} with a given level of confidence. Hence, we aim to build a set function C_{\alpha}^{n} defined on (\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{n}\times\mathcal{X} that will return a measurable subset of \mathcal{Y} for Y_{n+1} such that \mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n+1}\in C_{\alpha}^{n}\left((X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1\dots n};X_{n+1}\right)\right)\geq 1-\alpha\,. (3) Notice that in the above probability we have integrated over (X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1\dots n+1} .
The construction of C_{\alpha}^{n} stands on the notion of conformity. To begin with, we define a nonconformity score. It consists in a map S from \mathcal{U}^{n}\times\mathcal{U} to \mathbb{R} , symmetric in its first n arguments. The empirical distribution built from (S(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n};U_{j}))_{j=1,\ldots,n} will be the main tool to quantify how conformal to U_{1},\ldots,U_{n} , u\in\mathcal{U} is.
Archetypal scores in the usual regression framework with \mathcal{U}=\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell} are related to the magnitude of the empirical residual, the most popular example being S((X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n}),(x,y))=\|y-\widehat{f}(x)\|, (4) where \|\cdot\| is the Euclidean norm of \mathbb{R}^{\ell} .
Notice that in this last example the score function only depends on u=(x,y) but not on the calibration sample (U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}) . This will not be the case in the framework that we are developing in this paper (see the next section). Let \alpha\in(1/(n+1),1) . For the sake of conciseness, we will use the notation S(x,y)\coloneqq S((X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n});(x,y)) . For i=1,\ldots,n+1 , we will also write S_{i}\coloneqq S(X_{i},Y_{i}) .
Following for example [ [cross-reference] ] , the conformalized prediction set for Y_{n+1} when observing X_{n+1} is then C_{\alpha}^{n}(X_{n+1})\equiv C_{\alpha}^{n}((X_{1},Y_{1})\dots(X_{n},Y_{n});X_{n+1})=\left\{y\in\mathcal{Y}\;{:}\;S(X_{n+1},y)\leq S_{(n_{\alpha})}\right\}. (5) Here, n_{\alpha}\coloneqq\left\lceil(1-\alpha)(n+1)\right\rceil and S_{(1)}\leq\dots\leq S_{(n)} denotes the order statistics associated to (S_{i})_{i=1\dots n} . By construction, the above set C_{\alpha}^{n}(X_{n+1}) satisfies \displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n+1}\in C_{\alpha}^{n}(X_{n+1})\right)\geq 1-\alpha. (6) The key property ensuring that equation ( [cross-reference] ) holds is that (S_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,n+1} is exchangeable. Moreover, if the nonconformity scores (S_{i})_{i=1\dots n} have a continuous joint distribution, then we have the following upper bound, \displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n+1}\in C_{\alpha}^{n}(X_{n+1})\right)\leq 1-\alpha+\frac{1}{n+1}\,. (7) Returning to the regression framework and considering the residual score ( [cross-reference] ), the prediction set for Y_{n+1} given the covariable X_{n+1} is \displaystyle C_{\alpha}^{n}(X_{n+1})=B\left(\widehat{f}(X_{n+1}),S_{(n_{\alpha})}\right)\,, i.e. the ball with center \widehat{f}(X_{n+1}) and radius S_{(n_{\alpha})} .

3.2 Joint Mahalanobis score As discussed in the previous section, a useful score used in CI is the (squared) norm of the residual.
In this paper, we will consider a natural generalization of the previous score by considering some statistical evaluation of (R-\mu_{R})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}^{-1}(R-\mu_{R}) (assuming that everything here is well defined). Before explaining how we will estimate and handle the previous quantity, let it first bulk up
by including X in the score. For this purpose, let V\coloneqq(X^{\top}\ R^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p} and set \displaystyle\mathcal{S}=(V-\mu_{V})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}_{V}(V-\mu_{V}). (8) As before, we assume here that everything is well defined. The intuition behind this score is to work with standardized quantities. One may recognize in ( [cross-reference] ) the so-called Mahalanobis quadratic form, which in turn defines the Mahalanobis metric. This metric is classical in multivariate analysis and widely used for example for classification tasks (see [ [cross-reference] ] ).
The idea of including the covariate X in the score is inspired by the CI framework for time series, where X can contain the k previous residuals (see e.g. [ [cross-reference] ] , Section 4.2, or the end of this subsection) : the joint distribution (X^{\top}R^{\top})^{\top} is relevant for predicting the future \ell residuals (i.e. R ). In the context of CI, the position and covariance parameters of V are of course unknown or even undefined, and it is natural to replace them with their empirical estimators.
Nevertheless, in order to preserve exchangeability and so the finite sample theoretical guarantees provided by the classical CI procedure, one has to process such empirical estimators in an exchangeable fashion. Of course, another way to proceed would be to estimate these parameters separately, similarly to the pre-trained predictor, leading to a double split procedure. The first option is more challenging and this is our approach here. To begin with,
we now introduce precisely our working score. We first set V_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}X_{i}\\
R_{i}\end{pmatrix}\;(i=1,\ldots,n)\mbox{ and, for }z\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell},\;V_{n+1}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}X_{n+1}\\
z\end{pmatrix}. (9) Here, the variable z=y-\widehat{f}(X_{n+1}) (resp. y ) is used to locate the most likely potential residuals (resp. predictions) in the CI machinery.
We store the observation vectors V_{i},\;i=1\ldots,n and the guess V_{n+1}(z) in a matrix \mathbf{V}(z)\in\mathcal{M}_{n+1,p} whose first n row are the V_{i}^{\top},i=1,\ldots,n and the last one is V_{n+1}^{\top}(z) .
Following equation ( [cross-reference] ), we decompose \mathbf{V}(z) as \mathbf{V}(z)=(\mathbf{X}\ \mathbf{R}(z)) with \mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{M}_{n+1,k} and \mathbf{R}(z)\in\mathcal{M}_{n+1,\ell} . The empirically centered counterpart of \mathbf{V}(z) is \mathbf{W}(z)=\pi^{\perp}_{\mathbbm{1}}\mathbf{V}(z) .
We further compute the ridge empirical covariance matrix \widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\lambda}(z)\in\mathcal{M}_{p,p} , \displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{W}(z)^{\top}\mathbf{W}(z)+\lambda\mathbf{I}. (10) Here, \lambda>0 is the ridge parameter.
With all these supplementary notations, we are now able to define the score S_{i}(z)\;(1\leq i\leq n+1) as the square of the empirical Mahanalobis norm of W_{i}(z) (the i^{\mathrm{th}} row of \mathbf{W}(z) written in column). Namely, we have \displaystyle S_{i}(z)=\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\lambda}^{-1/2}(z)W_{i}(z)\|^{2}=W_{i}(z)^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\lambda}^{-1}(z)W_{i}(z). (11) It is readily seen that S_{i}(z) is the i\textsuperscript{th} diagonal element of the following matrix, \displaystyle\mathbf{S}(z) \displaystyle=\mathbf{W}(z)\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{W}(z)^{\top}\mathbf{W}(z)+\lambda\mathbf{I}\bigg)^{-1}\mathbf{W}(z)^{\top} \displaystyle=n\mathbf{W}(z)\Big(\mathbf{W}(z)^{\top}\mathbf{W}(z)+n\lambda\mathbf{I}\Big)^{-1}\mathbf{W}(z)^{\top}. (12) Remarkably, and contrarily to the norm residual score, the first n scores (S_{1}(z),\ldots,S_{n}(z)) also depend on z . This property induces a number of technical difficulties which are dealt with in Section [cross-reference] . To alleviate the notations we will sometimes omit the dependence in z in the matrix valued functions. First intuition on the confidence regions associated to S(z) Before continuing with the main results, let us consider the Gaussian case (X^{\top}R^{\top})^{\top}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma) . Anticipating Section [cross-reference] , we replace the ridge empirical covariance with \Sigma and set \lambda=0 . Then, setting T(z)=z-\Sigma_{21}\Sigma_{11}^{-1}X with z=y-\widehat{f}(X) and using the block inversion lemma (equation ( [cross-reference] )), our score amounts to \displaystyle S(z) \displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}X\\
z\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}X\\
z\end{pmatrix}=X^{\top}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{11})^{-1}X+T(z)^{\top}(\mathbf{\Sigma}/\mathbf{\Sigma}^{11})^{-1}T(z). (13) Assume also that the quantile of order 1-\alpha of S(R) (denoted by q_{1-\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}} ) is known, so that we can replace the empirical quantile usually computed in CI by its theoretical value q_{1-\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}} .
The confidence region becomes \displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{\infty} \displaystyle=\{z:X^{\top}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{11})^{-1}X+T(z)^{\top}(\mathbf{\Sigma}/\mathbf{\Sigma}^{11})^{-1}T(z)\leq q_{1-\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}\}. (14) Under our Gaussianity assumption, q_{1-\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}=q_{1-\alpha}(\chi^{2}(p)) . The ellipsoid \mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{\infty} has two important features : it is centered on the conditional expectation \mathbb{E}[R|X]=\Sigma_{21}\Sigma_{11}^{-1}X and its matrix is equal to the conditional covariance \mathrm{Cov}(R|X)=\mathbf{\Sigma}/\mathbf{\Sigma}^{11} . However, 
we can see in this simple case that \mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{\infty}=\varnothing\iff X^{\top}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{11})^{-1}X\geq q_{1-\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}} . This observation suggests the introduction of S^{\prime}(z) , an adjusted version of the score S(z) , defined as S^{\prime}(z)=S(z)-X^{\top}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{11})^{-1}X=T(z)^{\top}(\mathbf{\Sigma}/\mathbf{\Sigma}^{11})^{-1}T(z). (15) The score S^{\prime} is studied in Section [cross-reference] .
This new score leads to a second confidence region \mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\infty}=\{z:T(z)^{\top}(\mathbf{\Sigma}/\mathbf{\Sigma}^{11})^{-1}T(z)\leq q_{1-\alpha}^{\mathcal{F}}\}, (16) where q_{1-\alpha}^{\mathcal{F}}=q_{1-\alpha}(\chi^{2}(\ell)) in the Gaussian case. Contrarily to \mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{\infty} , this region is never empty as \Sigma_{21}\Sigma_{11}^{-1}X\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\infty} . In return, its finite sample counterpart \mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{n} can equal to \mathbb{R}^{\ell} (Section [cross-reference] ).
Observe finally that when working with the score matrix ( [cross-reference] ),
it is not obvious anymore that the prediction region built with the score matrix ( [cross-reference] ) is an ellipsoid, although we expect to obtain a prediction set similar to \mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{\infty} . Computation of the score Let us comment the effective computation of equation ( [cross-reference] ) before stating the upcoming Theorem [cross-reference] .
The evaluation of \mathbf{S}(z) in ( [cross-reference] ) is not directly tractable: for each candidate z , both a matrix inversion and matrix products have to be performed. Fortunately, it turns out that explicit inversion tricks can be used to overcome this first issue. First, notice that the matrix \mathbf{V}(z) is a linear function of z . More explicitly, we may write \mathbf{V}(z)=\mathbf{V}(0)+\mathbf{A}(z) , where \mathbf{A}(z) is the rank one matrix given by \displaystyle\mathbf{A}(z)=e_{n+1}\bigg(\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}z_{s}e_{k+s}\bigg)^{\top}=e_{n+1}(\mathbf{L}z)^{\top}, (17) where \mathbf{L}\in\mathcal{M}_{p,\ell} is the matrix such that \mathbf{L}^{\top}=(\mathbf{0}_{\ell,k}\ \vline\ \mathbf{I}_{\ell}) . Thus, \displaystyle\mathbf{W}(z)=\pi^{\perp}_{\mathbbm{1}}\mathbf{V}(0)+\pi^{\perp}_{\mathbbm{1}}e_{n+1}(\mathbf{L}z)^{\top}=\mathbf{W}(0)+v(\mathbf{L}z)^{\top}. (18) Here, \displaystyle v=\pi^{\perp}_{\mathbbm{1}}e_{n+1}=e_{n+1}-\frac{1}{n+1}\mathbbm{1}=\frac{1}{n+1}(-1-1\ldots-1\ n)^{\top},\ \ \ \text{with}\ \ \ \|v\|^{2}=\frac{n}{n+1}. (19) Equation ( [cross-reference] ) shows that \mathbf{W}(z) is a rank one perturbation of \mathbf{W}(0) . This crucial property is exploited in Lemma [cross-reference] , to prove our main Theorem [cross-reference] . Remark (Time series framework) . In the context of time series [ [cross-reference] , [cross-reference] ] , U_{i} takes the form U_{i}=(U_{i}^{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}} . Given a multi-horizon predictor (\widehat{U}_{t+1}^{i},\ldots,\widehat{U}_{t+\ell}^{i})=\widehat{f}(U_{t-k+1}^{i},\ldots,U_{t}^{i}) one then computes the time series of the residuals, R_{t}^{i}=U_{t}^{i}-\widehat{U}_{t}^{i} .
At a given time t , we aim at localizing with high probability the next \ell residuals, Y=(R_{t+1},\dots,R_{t+\ell}) using the k previous ones, X=(R_{t-k+1},\dots,R_{t}) . It may also happen that the time series and so the residuals are both multidimensional ( R_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} ). This is e.g. the case for time series describing the evolution of a position in \mathbb{R}^{3} ( d=3 ). In this case, X is a vector in \mathbb{R}^{kd} and Y as a vector in \mathbb{R}^{\ell d} . The vector corresponding to the i\textsuperscript{th} time series takes the form \displaystyle V_{i}=(X_{i}^{\top}\ Y_{i}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{dk+d\ell}. (20) This time series setting falls in the framework of this paper and will be developed extensively in [ [cross-reference] ] . The most direct interpretation of the score vector \text{diag}(\mathbf{S}(z))=(S_{1}(z),\ldots,S_{n+1}(z)) is the empirical Mahalanobis distance. This metric is e.g. also the central tool in linear discriminant analysis (LDA) [ [cross-reference] ] , Chapter 9-10. This is closely related to the Karhunen-Loève decomposition, which consists in finding an orthonormal basis (\xi_{i}\otimes\phi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{P})\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) on which a random field is naturally decomposed.
It is worth to notice that other interpretations in terms of leverage score and shape theory can also be made. The interested reader will find some insights in Appendix [cross-reference] .

3.3 The first ellipsoid \mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{n} : main theorem We first need to introduce some notations.
While the score \mathbf{S}(z) is built on a procedure which is exchangeable in the (n+1) -sample, we will in fact need quantities that are obtained only using the n sample (V_{1},\dots,V_{n}) . For this, we introduce the matrices \mathbf{B}_{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,p} , \widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,\lambda}\in\mathcal{M}_{p,p} and \mathbf{P}_{n,\lambda}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,n} such that \displaystyle(\mathbf{B}_{n})_{ij} \displaystyle\coloneqq(V_{i})_{j}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(V_{k})_{j},\ \ \widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,\lambda}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{B}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{n}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{p}=\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,\lambda}^{11}&\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}^{12}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}^{21}&\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,\lambda}^{22}\end{pmatrix}, (21) \displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{n,\lambda} \displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbf{B}_{n}(\mathbf{B}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{n}+n\lambda\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{n}^{\top}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{B}_{n}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{n}^{\top}. (22) Above, \widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,\lambda}^{11}\in\mathcal{M}_{k,k},\ \widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,\lambda}^{22}\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell,\ell},\ \widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}^{12}\in\mathcal{M}_{k,\ell}, and \widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}^{21}=(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}^{12})^{\top}\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell,k} . \mathbf{B}_{n} contains the data of the n -sample after being centered with their empirical mean, and \widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,\lambda} is the corresponding empirical ridge covariance matrix.
The matrix \mathbf{P}_{n,\lambda} is a regularized orthogonal projector, that is, \mathbf{P}_{n,0} is an orthogonal projector, \mathbf{P}_{n,\lambda}^{\top}=\mathbf{P}_{n,\lambda} and 0\preccurlyeq\mathbf{P}_{n,\lambda}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{P}_{n,0} for \lambda>0 . Denoting p_{i,n}=(\mathbf{P}_{n,\lambda})_{ii} ( p_{i,n}\in[0,1] ) we set \displaystyle q_{n,\alpha}\coloneqq np_{(n_{\alpha})}, (23) the n^{\text{th}}_{\alpha} order statistic of the n -tuple (np_{1,n},\dots,np_{n,n}) . Note that p_{(n_{\alpha})} is the empirical quantile of order (1-\alpha)(n+1)/n for the n -tuple (p_{1,n},\dots,p_{n,n}) , and not 1-\alpha (apply e.g. [ [cross-reference] ] , p. 305). Contrarily to standard n -samples, the p_{i,n} are not independent. For example, they are constrained by the following deterministic inequality: \sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i,n}=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{P}_{n,\lambda})\leq\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{P}_{n,0})=\text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_{n,0})\leq p . We can now state our first main result. Theorem 3.1 . Let \alpha\in(1/(n+1),1) . let ((X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})) \in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{n+1} exchangeable pairs of random vectors. If q_{n,\alpha}\geq n-1 , we set \mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{n}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{\ell} . If q_{n,\alpha}<n-1 , we define \mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{n} as the following ellipsoid, \displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{n}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}:(z-Z_{0}^{n})^{\top}\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{n}^{-1}(z-Z_{0}^{n})\leq\rho_{n,\alpha}\}, (24) where, setting \overline{X}_{n}\coloneqq n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\ \overline{R}_{n}\coloneqq n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i} and X_{n+1}^{\mathrm{c}}=X_{n+1}-\overline{X}_{n} , \displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{n} \displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,\lambda}/\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,\lambda}^{11}, (25) \displaystyle Z_{0}^{n} \displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}^{21}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,\lambda}^{11})^{-1}X_{n+1}^{\mathrm{c}}+\overline{R}_{n}, (26) \displaystyle\rho_{n,\alpha} \displaystyle=\frac{q_{n,\alpha}+1}{1-(q_{n,\alpha}+1)/n}-1-(X_{n+1}^{\mathrm{c}})^{\top}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,\lambda}^{11})^{-1}X_{n+1}^{\mathrm{c}}. (27) Then the set \mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{n} satisfies \displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1}-\widehat{Y}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{n})\geq 1-\alpha. (28) The ellipsoid in Theorem [cross-reference] is recovered by E_{\alpha}^{n}=\{\widehat{Y}_{n+1}\}+\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{n} , in the sense of the usual Minkowski set addition. An important feature of Lemma [cross-reference] is that \bm{\mathcal{A}}_{n} does not depend on V_{n+1} . Likewise, \rho_{n,\alpha} and Z_{0}^{n} depend on V_{n+1} only through X_{n+1} . Interestingly, when the distribution of V_{1} is elliptical (see Section [cross-reference] ) and V_{1} admits second order moment, the matrix \bm{\mathcal{A}}_{n} corresponds to an empirical estimate of the conditional covariance matrix \mathrm{Cov}(R_{1}|X_{1}) , up to a random positive multiplicative constant (see [ [cross-reference] ] , Theorem 1.5.4). In fact, one can see from equation ( [cross-reference] ) that only \rho_{n,\alpha}\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{n} is uniquely defined. This allows us to interpret \bm{\mathcal{A}}_{n} as an empirical conditional covariance matrix for such distributions, up to a rescaling of \rho_{n,\alpha} with the said multiplicative constant. Remark (Empirical linear conditional expectation) . Observe that Z_{0}^{n}=\widehat{e}_{n}+\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{n}X_{n+1} , where \displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{n}=\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}^{21}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,\lambda}^{11})^{-1},\ \ \widehat{e}_{n}=\overline{R}_{n}-\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{n}\overline{X}_{n}. In comparison, the (full) conditional expectation is obtained by minimizing the map T(f)=\mathbb{E}[\|R-f(X)\|^{2}] over all measurable maps f . Finally, \bm{\mathcal{A}}_{n} and (\widehat{e}_{n},\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{n}) are linked together by \min T_{n}=T_{n}(\widehat{e}_{n},\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{n})=\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{n})-\ell\lambda . In the previous theorem, the introduced objects are not exchangeable in terms of the (n+1) -sample.
