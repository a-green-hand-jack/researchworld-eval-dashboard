Outcome Reversal Audits for Pairwise Leaderboards:
Exact Balanced Cases and Matched Design Evidence
Anonymous Authors1
Abstract
Pairwise leaderboards summarize heterogeneous
comparison designs with a common ranked list,
but a small fraction of changed outcomes need
not represent a comparable perturbation across de-
signs. We audit fixed-schedule outcome reversals
in public crowdsourced, expert, model-judge, and
basketball data, combining influence-guided se-
lection with full Bradley–Terry refits and random-
corruption controls. An elementary balance con-
dition yields an exact minimum reversal budget,
providing a reference case for approximate au-
dits and enabling identical-schedule comparisons
of human and GPT-4 judgments. The observed
top-two reversal budgets range from one record
in Arena to 61 in full MT-Bench GPT-4 data;
the latter is certified exact, while the unbalanced-
data budgets are feasible witnesses. Across 500
matched balanced panels, the mean exact budget
for reversing GPT-4 versus Claude is 17.82 hu-
man judgments versus 35.79 GPT-4 judgments,
although this conditional difference does not es-
tablish a causal effect of judge type. Random flips,
cluster resampling, alternative challengers, and
tie policies expose different failure modes, sup-
porting audits that report the comparison design,
uncertainty, and edit unit alongside a percentage.
1. Introduction
Pairwise evaluation asks which of two participants per-
formed better and aggregates the answers into scores. Its
appeal spans crowdsourced model evaluation, expert bench-
marks, automated judging, and sports. A ranking, however,
compresses several quantities: the fitted score margin, the
comparison schedule, the available outcomes, and the un-
certainty from observing a particular sample. Two adjacent

We study an explicit counterfactual: keep the participants
and observed pairings fixed, and reverse the outcomes of a
small number of decisive records. This represents a sensitiv-
ity analysis of recorded labels, whether errors are accidental
or selected deliberately. It does not assume the ability to
submit votes to a live platform, alter prompts, or change a
real sporting result. For basketball the intervention is on the
historical results table, not an operational threat model.
Three questions guide the study. How different are selected
reversals and uniformly random reversals of the same size?
How much of an apparent cross-design contrast survives
matching the evaluated model pairs and questions? When
can a computed edit budget be interpreted as an exact ro-
bustness radius, rather than a successful perturbation found
by a heuristic?
Our contribution is a controlled audit of these questions,
rather than a new general influence-function algorithm.
First, we specialize the perturbation algebra to outcome
reversals, whose objective changes by an exactly linear term.
We state and prove a balanced-schedule condition under
which score ordering equals total-win ordering, so a discrete
minimum budget is available without a first-order approxi-
mation. Second, we use the public MT-Bench data to build
paired human/GPT-4 panels with identical, balanced com-
parison schedules. Third, we place these results beside
public Arena and two NBA seasons, reporting random cor-
ruption, cluster uncertainty, alternative challengers, baseline
selection rules, and tie-policy sensitivity.
The empirical contrasts resist a single claim that leader-
boards are either stable or unstable. Arena’s one-record
reversal concerns a near-zero margin whose bootstrap inter-
val includes zero. MT-Bench’s larger budgets coexist with
complete resistance to the tested random corruption budgets.
In NBA 2015 the runner-up is not the cheapest tested chal-
lenger. Even the denominator changes the interpretation:
the fraction of all games can be small while the fraction
relative to comparisons involving the two focal participants

2. Related Work
Chiang et al. (2024) describe Arena, the use of pairwise mod-
els, and uncertainty in preference-based rankings. Zheng
et al. (2023) introduce MT-Bench and compare expert and
model judgments, including limitations of model judges.
Our common score model facilitates a sensitivity compari-
son; it does not reconstruct every official leaderboard adjust-
ment or equate human preference with sporting strength.
Influence functions approximate how fitted quantities
change under data perturbations (Koh & Liang, 2017). Brod-
erick et al. (2020) use this approach to assess sensitivity to
dropping a small fraction of observations, stressing that
conventional uncertainty and worst-case sensitivity answer
different questions. We use the same general differentiation
principle, but the intervention changes outcomes at fixed
sample size and fixed pair counts.
The closest supplied ranking study, Huang et al. (2025), eval-
uates deletion sensitivity of Bradley–Terry rankings, includ-
ing Arena and MT-Bench and human/model judgments. We
do not claim that influential comparisons or cross-platform
sensitivity are new discoveries. Our distinct evidence con-
cerns fixed-schedule reversals, an exact balanced reference
case, and paired panels that remove differences in compar-
ison counts and selected question cells. Deletion changes
the schedule and can destroy the balance property on which
our exact result depends; its budgets cannot be numerically
compared to reversal budgets as if the edit units were inter-
changeable.
Min et al. (2025) study vote rigging through new votes and
show that comparisons not involving a target can affect
its ranking. Our full influence rule likewise permits indi-
rect effects, but here the recorded comparisons are fixed.
In our five main top-two audits, endpoint-restricted selec-
tion matches unrestricted selection. Thus the availability
of indirect effects does not imply a benefit from exploiting
them in every dataset. We do not make a priority claim for
the familiar balanced-design ordering property; the exact-
budget construction is used as an interpretable diagnostic
and experimental control.

4. Data and Experimental Design
Public data.
We use the 57,477-record Arena human-
preference snapshot, both released MT-Bench splits, and the
2014 and 2015 NBA regular seasons from FiveThirtyEight’s
historical results (FiveThirtyEight, 2025). Dataset commits,
file hashes, selection code, and licenses are recorded in the
reproducibility package. All source files matched the spec-
ified SHA-256 values. We use all Arena participants and
records, with no score- or exposure-based inclusion thresh-
old. MT-Bench has six participants and 80 questions; its
human split includes expert and author annotators. Each
GPT-4 pair has 160 records, making the full schedule bal-
anced. Its 380 “tie (inconsistent)” records are treated as
half-wins within the 600 total ties. Sensitivity checks omit
them and, separately, omit all ties.
For NBA we retain the NBA league, non-playoff games,
the two stated season years, and iscopy = 0, obtaining
one row per game. We do not use Elo scores, point mar-
gins, or forecasts. Each season has 30 teams, rather than
pooling franchises across eras. This keeps participants and
the season window interpretable. The model omits home
advantage and changing team strength, so its ranking is a
static aggregate, not a reconstruction of official standings or
a sports prediction system.
Primary targets and budgets.
The primary audit com-
pares the original first and second ranks. The pairs are
GPT-4-1106-preview versus GPT-4-0125-preview in Arena,
GPT-4 versus Claude-v1 in both MT-Bench splits, San An-
tonio versus Oklahoma City in NBA 2014, and Golden State
versus Atlanta in NBA 2015. We additionally audit every
challenger to the original leader and all adjacent pairs within
the original top ten (all participants for MT-Bench): 163
distinct pair problems. Main top-pair searches permit up to
20% of records, and broader searches up to 5%; all observed
primary crossings occur below 5%. A missing crossing is
recorded as censored.
Random corruption uses 500 independent subsets at each
rounded-up budget in {0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5}% of all
records, plus the observed primary crossing budget. Dupli-
cate integer budgets are merged. We record original-leader
loss, original-pair reversal, and Kendall’s τ over all fitted
scores. Wilson intervals describe Monte Carlo uncertainty
of loss probabilities. This random design selects from deci-
sive rows, while its x-axis counts edits relative to all rows;
the distinction matters when tie rates differ.

Resampling uncertainty.
We draw 1,000 cluster boot-
strap samples per dataset, using whole MT-Bench ques-
tions, exact Arena prompt-string hashes, and NBA calendar
dates as clusters. The clusters are sampled with replace-
ment, retaining all records in each sampled cluster. Intervals
describe the original named pair without reselecting it in
each resample. They do not adjust for selecting the origi-
nal winner and are not simultaneous rank confidence sets.
User/session identifiers are unavailable in the Arena CSV,
and identical text does not capture all shared-user depen-
dence. NBA date clustering also does not capture depen-
dence across a team’s season. We therefore use these inter-
vals as conditional uncertainty diagnostics, not population-
level guarantees.
Matched schedules.
To compare the two MT-Bench judg-
ment designs, a cell is an unordered model pair, question,
and turn. There are 1,814 cells observed by both designs.
The minimum shared-cell count over the 15 pairs is 99. For
each of 500 panels, we uniformly select 99 shared cells with-
out replacement within each pair, then choose one available
human judgment per selected cell uniformly; GPT-4 sup-
plies its unique corresponding record. Both panels thus con-
tain 1,485 records with exactly the same pair/question/turn
schedule and 99 records per pair. Human annotators are not
forced to match across cells. The balance proposition gives
exact pair and leader radii for both panels.
We fix GPT-4 versus Claude-v1 as the paired contrast across
panels, and also report the minimum over all challengers.
Panel quantiles summarize variation from this subsampling
and annotator selection procedure; they are not population
confidence intervals. Questions, response pairs, and the
available human annotations remain those of the original
dataset. Matching removes measured schedule differences,
not possible selection bias or judge self-preference.
Implementation and checks.
All numerical experiments
use CPU double precision and a recorded base seed,
20261028, with deterministic offsets for separate proce-
dures. The main experiment took 93.1 seconds with single-
threaded numerical libraries on an Intel Xeon Gold 6230
host. We checked the optimizer against independent BFGS
optimization, the derivative against finite differences, and
the balanced formula against exhaustive subset enumeration
on a small synthetic unit test. No synthetic data are used as
empirical evidence. We numerically checked both sides of
every exact full-GPT pair threshold and all matched-panel
focal thresholds. Code and generated tables are included.
5. Results
5.1. A small fraction has several meanings
Table 2 shows substantial variation in the selected top-pair
budgets: one Arena record, 46 MT human records, 61 MT
GPT-4 records, and four or eight NBA games. These are
changes to the analyzed frozen tables, not assertions of
past manipulation. For Arena the result is also a trivial
exact minimum for the fitted pair: no edit leaves its strict
initial order unchanged, and one edit reverses it. For other
unbalanced cases, we do not establish optimality.
Exposure normalization changes the scale of the comparison.
NBA 2014 requires 0.3252% of all season games but 2.50%
relative to the two teams’ comparison exposure; the MT
human values are 1.3711% and 2.52%, respectively. Their
global fractions differ appreciably, while their exposure
fractions nearly coincide. This does not make the designs
equivalent: opponents, outcome margins, and dependence
remain different. It does show why a global percentage
alone can exaggerate a contrast. Arena remains a particu-
larly small-budget case under either denominator.
The small Arena budget also needs its uncertainty context.
Its original gap is approximately 0.004 log-odds, with a
bootstrap interval spanning zero and only 52.8% retention
of the original leader. The two MT-Bench gaps have positive
bootstrap intervals in our resampling procedure. Both NBA
intervals span zero despite their differing point margins and
edit budgets. These diagnostics reinforce that a change in
displayed rank can concern an uncertain distinction rather
than a large change in assessed performance.

5.3. The easiest challenger can be third
For NBA 2015, seven selected reversals put Houston, orig-
inally third, above Golden State, while eight are needed
along the chosen path for Atlanta, originally second. This
is a strictly better found witness, not a proof that seven is
globally minimal. In all other primary datasets the smallest
found leader-loss budget coincides with the top-pair budget.
The direct-head-to-head baseline fails to reverse either NBA
primary pair using its available helpful outcomes: San Anto-
nio has no recorded win over Oklahoma City in these season
records, while the available Golden State win over Atlanta is
insufficient. Endpoint-restricted, unrestricted influence, and
raw-win-gain rules all achieve the same primary crossing
counts (Table 3). This is a useful negative result: the more
general selection rule offers no count advantage on these
five main pairs. Neither the existence of graph-mediated
influence nor the algorithm’s sophistication substitutes for
checking the actual schedule and outcome supply.
5.4. Balanced matching preserves a judge contrast
The full GPT-4 split has an exact focal and leader-loss ra-
dius of 61, certified by Proposition 1. Its total-win margin
between GPT-4 and Claude-v1 is 121; 60 helpful direct re-
versals leave a positive margin, and 61 reverse it. A question-
cluster bootstrap that preserves this complete schedule gives
a percentile interval of [39.0, 82.0] for the focal radius,
showing that an exact radius on the observed table can still
vary under resampling.

This is not evidence that automated judging is generally
more reliable. A larger margin can reflect stronger consen-

et al. (2023). The matched design does not separate these
explanations. It identifies an outcome-level contrast for
the released response pairs, conditional on their selection.
Moreover, the focal radius and the full leader radius some-
times differ in human panels; both summaries are reported
in Table 4.
5.5. Approximation and policy choices matter
Linear prediction is close at some primary thresholds but
is not uniformly exact: it predicts 56 edits rather than the
certified 61 in full GPT-4 data and seven rather than eight for
the NBA 2015 top pair. Among 163 broader pair problems,
158 cross within the allowed searches; the remaining 5 are
censored. Among the crossed cases, absolute threshold
error reaches 98 records. Per-dataset median relative errors
range from about 3% to 23% (Table 5). These are correlated
audits within five datasets, not independent evidence for
a universal error distribution. Approximation is useful for
selecting records, but reporting its predicted crossing as a
measured reversal would misstate results.
Tie handling has a larger qualitative effect than numerical
precision. Dropping all Arena ties reverses its original top
pair before any further edit; thus its original one-record find-
ing is conditional on the tie policy. Omitting all ties changes
the MT human and GPT-4 thresholds for their original pairs
to 42 and 39, respectively. Omitting only GPT-4’s inconsis-
tent ties gives 47. Those omissions change pair counts, so
the balanced optimum for the full dataset no longer applies.
The global percentage ordering of the full MT splits per-
sists in these particular omission checks, but the raw count
ordering does not persist when all ties are omitted.
Changing λ from the default to 10−5 or 0.1 leaves all five
main counts unchanged; λ = 1 increases the Arena count
to two and leaves the other counts unchanged. Restricting
MT humans to expert-labeled annotators gives 41 edits on
2,668 records; removing the one repeated annotation key
leaves the primary threshold at 46. Complete ablation re-
sults appear in Appendix B. These checks support some
local robustness of the comparisons while also identifying a
concrete failure of tie-policy invariance.
6. Discussion and Limitations
This study supports reporting a profile of stability: the edit
operation and eligible rows, a focal pair or leader-loss tar-
get, the observed gap, the comparison exposure, random-
corruption behavior, and refitted witnesses. An uncertainty
interval and a selected-edit budget should accompany each
other because neither implies the other. The balanced case
is particularly transparent: exact robustness is a property of
the observed win margin and available reversal gains, not a
claim about a judge’s truthfulness.

This work studies the reliability of aggregate rankings and
provides reproducible sensitivity diagnostics. It can help
evaluation providers avoid overstating small differences and

B.2. Interpretation of uncertainty and negative results
The primary targeted counts are deterministic conditional on the dataset, penalty, selection rule, and row-order tie breaking.
They therefore do not need a Monte Carlo confidence interval. Uncertainty about the data sample is instead assessed by
the cluster gap bootstrap and, for the complete GPT-4 schedule, the exact-radius bootstrap. The repeated matched panels
quantify sensitivity to a specified construction rule. Reporting these intervals as interchangeable would overstate inferential
precision.
All successful thresholds are verified with before/after refits. No fit failed to converge in the reported experiments. The direct
NBA baseline failures and the five capped broad searches are retained. We did not attempt exact combinatorial optimization
for arbitrary schedules; a better selection rule might improve an unbalanced upper bound. We also did not generate fresh
