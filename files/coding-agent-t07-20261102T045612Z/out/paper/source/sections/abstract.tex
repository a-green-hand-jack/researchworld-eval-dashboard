\begin{abstract}
Evaluating a language model on a full benchmark is costly, so many methods run a model on a small item subset and predict its full-benchmark score using item-level results of previously evaluated ``source'' models. We study this in a controlled factorial design on 13 binary item-level score matrices (Open LLM Leaderboard v2 and HELM): six item selectors (random, difficulty-stratified, anchor clusters, IRT-anchors, D-optimal, max-variance) crossed with plain-mean, regression, kNN and IRT-based estimators, under an IID split of models and two \emph{extrapolation} splits where the models to be evaluated are more accurate than all sources. With 30 replicate splits per cell and bootstrap intervals, we find: under IID splits, model-based estimators reduce RMSE by 17--29\% relative to a random-subset mean ($k{=}50$) and improve Spearman correlation by about 0.04, but selecting items by clustering or by information without a model-based estimator is at best marginally better than random (Anchor-IRT, 0.90) and otherwise worse; under extrapolation every method that relies on the source models for its point estimate is 15--140\% \emph{worse} than a random-subset mean, with a bias that grows with the target--source accuracy gap. A per-target two-parameter recalibration removes most of this bias but only reaches parity with the random mean (0.96), and with held-out model families but no strength shift model-based estimators work again. Methods that keep item sampling random are robust: source-difficulty stratification lowers RMSE by 12--16\% in all regimes. We also study a cross-fitted finite-population difference estimator (CF), which we prove unbiased under simple random sampling and verify empirically (no detectable bias; 92--94\% coverage of Wald intervals), and an empirical-Bayes cohort shrinkage variant (CF-EB) that gets 25\% lower RMSE than random in IID and 12--16\% under extrapolation, at the price of a small bias. No estimator improved the rank correlation under extrapolation by more than 0.005 at $k\ge50$ (at most 0.04 at smaller $k$). Gains of safe methods are modest, and we discuss the limits of this evidence.
\end{abstract}
