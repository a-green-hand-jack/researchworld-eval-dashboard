BEYOND TOKEN BOUNDARIES:
BYTE-LEVEL PRE-
DICTION FROM TOKENIZED LANGUAGE MODELS
Anonymous authors
Paper under double-blind review
ABSTRACT
Tokenized language models condition on discrete encodings, but users supply text
that may end inside a token. Conditioning on a single encoding can therefore
disagree with conditioning on the observed bytes, and different model vocabu-
laries complicate probability-level ensembling. We formulate byte prediction as
marginalization over token histories that first cover the observed prefix, yielding
an exact representation of the byte process induced by a token model without
weight updates. We derive incremental inference and a reverse-search specializa-
tion, making explicit the support assumptions required for tokenizer-based prun-
ing. On HumanEval Random Span, byte-level prediction raises CodeLlama-7b’s
suffix-prefix-middle greedy pass rate from 45.0 to 63.9 and its reported pass@10
from 66.5 to 84.3. Averaging predictions in byte space reaches 65.4 on MMLU
and 42.1 pass@1 on HumanEval, exceeding the best reported individual scores of
63.4 and 38.4. Benefits are not universal: individual-model code generation can
regress, and the GSM8K ensemble underperforms its strongest member. These
results support byte-space conditioning for boundary-sensitive completion and
vocabulary-independent ensembling, while leaving runtime and approximation er-
ror unresolved.

INTRODUCTION
Subword tokenization provides a practical vocabulary for language modeling, including representa-
tions of rare words Sennrich et al. (2016). Yet a token boundary is an internal modeling choice, not
a constraint on the text a user can provide. A code completion prompt may stop halfway through an
identifier, and the tokenization of that partial identifier may differ from the corresponding prefix of
the tokenization of the completed program. A model asked to continue the former token history can
consequently make a different prediction from the one obtained by conditioning its underlying text
distribution on the observed prefix.
We call this discrepancy tokenization bias. Here, bias denotes a difference between two condition-
ing operations on the same model, not social bias or necessarily a discrepancy from the true data
distribution. The distinction matters: recovering the byte distribution induced by a model does not
correct errors already present in that model. Nevertheless, the supplied synthetic experiments exhibit
a particularly sharp boundary failure: a valid next byte receives zero probability under deterministic
token-history conditioning even though its data-generating probability is positive.
Our approach retains the token model and changes inference. For an observed byte prefix, we
consider all token histories whose final token first reaches or crosses its endpoint. These cover
encodings partition the event of observing that prefix. Their probabilities give the prefix likelihood
and, by taking ratios, the next-byte distribution. Crucially, a covering token may extend beyond the
prompt: restricting the sum to tokenizations that end exactly at the prompt boundary would omit
precisely the hypotheses needed for mid-token completion.
This formulation also resolves an interface problem in ensembling. Token probabilities from dis-
tinct vocabularies cannot be directly averaged by token index. Byte probabilities can: each model
contributes a distribution over the same 256 byte values, together with a termination event. Figure 1
summarizes both applications.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it gives a byte-token representation lemma and separates
unconditional exactness of the representation from assumptions needed to prune token histories.
Second, it specifies incremental marginalization and a reverse-search specialization that reuse token-
model predictions without retraining. Third, it evaluates boundary-sensitive infilling, individual-
model prediction, and heterogeneous ensembling using the recorded experiments. The strongest
evidence concerns suffix-prefix-middle (SPM) infilling; mixed results elsewhere delimit the claim.
We do not claim that byte-level decoding uniformly improves accuracy or that exact marginalization
is inexpensive for every token model.

RELATED WORK
Tokenization and segmentation.
Subword units address vocabulary coverage in neural language
processing Sennrich et al. (2016). Tokenizer choices also affect pretraining and domain adaptation
Dagan et al. (2024). These studies motivate treating the tokenizer as part of the modeling interface
rather than an interchangeable text preprocessing step. Marginalization over alternative segmen-
tations has an established role in language modeling: Grave et al. (2019) trains hybrid models by
summing over segmentations. Our focus is instead inference with a fixed pretrained token model,
including histories whose last token overshoots an observed byte prefix. The representation iden-
tity is a probability decomposition; efficient evaluation requires additional structure in the model’s
support.
Infilling and boundary repair.
Fill-in-the-middle training rearranges text to support generation
conditioned on both a prefix and a suffix Bavarian et al. (2022). Code Llama includes models with
infilling capabilities Rozi`ere et al. (2023). Boundary repair is directly relevant when the supplied
prefix ends inside a subword. Token healing backs up from the boundary and constrains regener-
ation to match the observed text; Dagan et al. (2024) discusses both the motivation and variants
of this procedure. Token alignment uses character matching for subword completion Athiwaratkun
et al. (2024). Both provide relevant empirical baselines. Our mathematical target is the full model-
induced byte conditional, which averages compatible histories according to their probability rather
than selecting a single repaired history. The experimental records do not specify the exact healing
or alignment implementation, so the comparisons apply to the recorded configurations.
Evaluation and heterogeneous models.
HumanEval Chen et al. (2021) and MBPP Austin et al.
(2021) evaluate program synthesis. MMLU Hendrycks et al. (2020), PIQA Bisk et al. (2020), Nat-
ural Questions Kwiatkowski et al. (2019), TriviaQA Joshi et al. (2017), and GSM8K Cobbe et al.
(2021) cover knowledge, physical reasoning, question answering, and mathematical problem solv-
ing. We use the results recorded for these benchmarks to examine whether boundary correction
transfers beyond infilling. Our ensemble uses an arithmetic mean of byte conditionals, providing

Call a token history canonical when E(D(h)) = h. A deterministic tokenizer may never emit a
noncanonical sequence, but that fact alone does not imply P(h) = 0 for a learned token model.
Discarding such a sequence changes the represented distribution if its probability is positive.

Figure 2 illustrates the specialization. Its candidate count is at most L|V|, before using prefix in-
dexing. This is not a bound on end-to-end neural inference time: evaluating history probabilities
and obtaining their next-token logits also costs computation. For unrestricted models, the number
of compatible histories can grow exponentially with prefix length. Thus the exact representation
for arbitrary token models should be distinguished from an efficient implementation under restricted
support.

Under canonical support, at most one positive-mass history ends exactly at a particular byte prefix,
namely E(x). The incremental step then requires at most one new next-token distribution, while
overshooting covers contribute already determined bytes. This does not imply one model call per
generated token, or a measured speedup. Without canonical support, distinct boundary histories
require distinct model states and predictions.
Byte-greedy and token-greedy decoding also optimize different local choices. Even at an unam-
biguous token boundary, the sum of probabilities of several tokens sharing a first byte can exceed
the probability of the single most likely token. A gain from byte-greedy decoding therefore need
not demonstrate removal of a mid-token error. Truncating histories or using a beam to approxi-
mate marginalization is not exact in general; the recorded beam experiment does not specify enough
implementation detail to equate beam width with retained probability mass.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
SETUP AND REPORTING SCOPE
The experimental record covers first- and third-order Markov chains, a six-hidden-layer GPT-2 ar-
chitecture trained on synthetic data, and pretrained Transformer language models. General-purpose
evaluations use Llama2-7B, Yi-1.5-6B, and Mistral-7B-v0.3. Infilling uses CodeLlama-7b. The
code-generation table labels its models CodeLlama2-7b, CodeLlama2-13b, and Yi-Coder-1.5B; we
preserve those labels because checkpoint identifiers are not supplied and the 7B naming differs
across experiments.
Infilling is evaluated on HumanEval Random Span Bavarian et al. (2022) in prefix-suffix-middle
(PSM) and suffix-prefix-middle (SPM) modes. The baselines are ordinary token generation, heal-
ing, and alignment; the latter two are reported only for SPM. We reproduce greedy pass rates and
the supplied @10 scores. The provided infilling figure specifies that @10 is the maximum over
a temperature sweep, rather than a score at one fixed temperature. The grid, sampling seeds, and
sample-count details are unavailable.
The general benchmarks are MMLU, PIQA, NQ, TriviaQA, and GSM8K; code generation uses Hu-
manEval and MBPP. All reported scores are percentages. We retain the logs’ accuracy and pass@1
terminology without assuming unreported answer extraction or normalization rules. Ensembles av-
erage byte probabilities of the top two models. The record does not establish how the top two
were selected, whether selection used held-out data, or all task-specific pair identities. It explic-
itly identifies Yi-Coder-1.5B and CodeLlama2-13b for HumanEval, and discusses Yi-1.5-6B with
Mistral-7B-v0.3 for GSM8K.
The records provide aggregate results, not example-level outputs, repeated runs, uncertainty inter-
vals, compute measurements, or complete inference configurations. Accordingly, numerical dif-
ferences below are descriptive; we make no statistical significance claim. Missing entries denote
unreported measurements, not zero performance. Appendix D lists the principal reproducibility
gaps.
4.2
SYNTHETIC VERIFICATION
The first-order example uses byte symbols A and B and a vocabulary containing A, B, and AA
(Figure 3). The record reports a zero token-conditioned probability for A following context A,
despite a byte transition probability 1 −α. A token history ending in standalone A can exclude
futures that would instead have merged that byte into AA. Byte-prefix conditioning retains those
histories through a cover token that straddles the observed boundary.
The third-order experiment reports severe predictive distortion under token conditioning and recov-
ery of the chain’s original conditional probabilities after conversion. The supplied plot supports
qualitative agreement between corrected and ground-truth predictions across the displayed contexts.
The record does not include numeric errors, learned parameters, chain coefficients, training hyper-
parameters, or repeated runs. We therefore use these experiments as a qualitative mechanism check,
not a quantified accuracy or calibration guarantee for arbitrary neural models.

CODE INFILLING AND BOUNDARY SENSITIVITY
Table 1 shows the clearest effect. Standard token-level greedy decoding drops from 64.9 in PSM

CONCLUSION
Byte-prefix conditioning provides a principled interface between tokenized language models and
arbitrary text boundaries. Minimal cover histories yield an exact representation of a token model’s
induced byte distribution; reverse search and incremental reuse make the computation structured,
with efficient canonical pruning requiring a separate support assumption. The experimental evi-
dence is strongest for SPM code infilling, where byte-level prediction recovers most of the degra-
dation relative to PSM. A shared byte space also enables ensembles that improve several recorded
benchmarks.
The results establish neither universal accuracy gains nor inexpensive exact inference for unre-
stricted neural models. Individual-model regressions, the GSM8K ensemble loss, missing uncer-
tainty estimates, and unmeasured pruning error limit stronger claims. Future work should measure
noncanonical probability mass and inference cost, specify reproducible decoding protocols, and
evaluate ensemble weighting using held-out selection. The essential benefit is a coherent condition-
ing and aggregation space; its computational and task-level value must be assessed separately.

PROBABILITY IDENTITIES AND EXACTNESS
Finiteness and disjointness.
For n > 0, each token in a cover history contributes at least one
byte, so its token count is at most n. With finite V, the set in equation 5 is finite. Its cardinality
can nevertheless grow exponentially in n. If one cover history were a strict token-prefix extension
of another, its penultimate decoding would already contain n bytes, contradicting minimality. Thus
the corresponding token-cylinder events are pairwise disjoint. This establishes the sum without
assuming a unique tokenization of any byte string.
