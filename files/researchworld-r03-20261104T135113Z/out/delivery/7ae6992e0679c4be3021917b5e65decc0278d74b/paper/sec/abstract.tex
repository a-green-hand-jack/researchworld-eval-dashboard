\begin{abstract}
Evaluating a new model on every benchmark of a leaderboard is costly, but historical score matrices of earlier models are available. We study the cold-start question: given such a model $\times$ benchmark matrix with missing cells and a new model that can run only $k$ benchmarks, which $k$ should it run, how should the other scores be inferred, and how large must $k$ be? We evaluate on held-out models, with selection and all fitting restricted to training models, a fixed target set so that every $k$ is scored on the same cells, bootstrap intervals over held-out models (checked against organisation- and family-clustered resampling), and a criterion for ``enough'' fixed in advance (the fraction of the gap between the column mean and a full-information predictor that is closed). The quantitative answers come mainly from embedding models (MTEB, nearly complete blocks of up to 275 tasks) under random 10-fold splits; five generative-LLM matrices ($N=6$--$57$) give short curves. On a sparse MTEB matrix ($30\%$ observed) about $16$ benchmarks are needed and informed selection no longer beats random ones from $k=16$. On MTEB (152 models $\times$ 56 tasks, random folds) four informed benchmarks give a normalised error of $0.20$--$0.22$ against $0.30$ for random ones and $1.04$ for the column mean, and greedy selection meets the gap-closed criterion at $k=2$--$4$ (for $80\%$) and $k=4$--$16$ (for $90\%$) over two selectors and two inference rules, on a coarse grid of $k$ (random selection: $5$--$8$ and $12$--$16$). Benchmarks chosen by conditional variance or log-determinant beat random ones at small $k$, whereas leverage-score, pivoted-QR and highest-variance selection are worse than random; the advantage shrinks to $\le0.03$ nMSE by $k\approx16$ and no selector is uniformly best (forward ridge and an adaptive greedy rule gain a further $0.03$--$0.04$). With whole organisations held out the floors rise (from $0.15$--$0.18$ to $0.27$--$0.33$), the advantage of informed selection is gone for $k\ge16$, and which informed selector needs fewest benchmarks changes with the split; inference fails for the newest models in a temporal split, where release-date terms do not help, but a Mahalanobis check on the revealed scores flags the MTEB-56 models with $3$--$11\times$ the error. Bounds for random subsets and a submodularity guarantee for greedy selection describe the shape of the curves, not their level on real leaderboards. All numbers come from the shipped code.
\end{abstract}
