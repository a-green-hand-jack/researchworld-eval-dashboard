Abstract
Small fractions of edited comparisons can change a fitted leaderboard, but the1
fraction alone hides who is affected and whether the original leader was distinguish-2
able. We audit this interpretation using fixed public snapshots of crowdsourced3
model preferences, curated human and GPT-4 judgments, and three NBA seasons.4
Outcome reversals are selected by standard influence scores and validated by full5
Bradley–Terry refitting; random edits and influence-based deletion are controls.6
We report both the global edit fraction and the maximum fraction of any entrant’s7
comparisons touched. At a nominal 1% global budget, uncapped outcome rever-8
sals switch the original top-pair ordering in 4 of 6 full cohorts. The same edits9
can touch substantially more than 1% of one entrant’s record. Requiring each10
entrant’s exposure to stay within 1% leaves only 1 observed reversal, in an Arena11
cohort whose original margin is already compatible with zero. Integer feasibility12
accounts for much of the NBA contrast; it is not evidence of certified robustness.13
Matched human–judge panels and specification checks further separate initial score14
separation from perturbation response. The contribution is an exposure-aware,15
uncertainty-explicit empirical audit, not a new influence method, optimal attack, or16
causal comparison of evaluation designs.17
1 Introduction18
Pairwise leaderboards compress heterogeneous evidence into a simple ordering. A model can be19
advertised as first, even when its fitted advantage is smaller than ordinary sampling variation, and a20
change to a small fraction of the entire comparison archive can be described as a small disturbance to21
that model’s evidence. Neither interpretation follows automatically. The total archive, an entrant’s22
incident comparisons, and the experimental unit need not be interchangeable denominators.23
Existing research already establishes that influential preference deletions can change Bradley–Terry24
(BT) model rankings, including on Arena and MT-Bench [ 3]. Influence-function tools [ 4] and25
approximate maximum influence perturbations (AMIP) [1] provide efficient ways to find such subsets.26
Therefore, showing another low-budget flip or applying an inverse Hessian to a BT margin is not our27
contribution. Our question is narrower: what does a global fragility number conceal about individual28
exposure, feasible perturbations, and original uncertainty?29
This matters even without a malicious adversary. Random errors, removal of questionable votes,30
systematic changes to a judging policy, and targeted relabeling are different operations. Concentrating31
changes on a single entrant can use a small global fraction while modifying a much larger share32
of that entrant’s evidence. Conversely, an integer interpretation of a small per-entrant percentage33

We analyze three observational evaluation designs: crowdsourced Arena preferences, curated MT-36
Bench judgments (human and model judge), and fixed-season professional basketball outcomes.37
These designs differ in participant count, comparison density, ties, task sampling, and dependence.38
They are not randomized treatments. Our results establish conditional differences between the39

The audit has three components. First, each exact-refit witness is accompanied by a global fraction, re-42
alized per-entrant exposure, and actual edit count. Second, strict incident-exposure caps are evaluated43
alongside unconstrained influence selection, with integer-linear optimization checking the capped44
surrogate and feasibility bounds separating arithmetic restrictions from failed searches. Third, original45
cluster-bootstrap uncertainty and random-edit variation are kept distinct from adversarial response;46
a common-cell MT comparison controls the comparison schedule without equating aggregate cells47
with individual votes.48
The resulting message is conditional rather than uniformly alarmist. Global low-budget flips occur, but49
near-ties and concentrated exposure change their meaning. The strict-cap comparison is informative50
only together with the feasible number of edits. No failed search in this paper is a robustness51
certificate. All modifications are offline counterfactuals to published records, not interventions on a52
live leaderboard.53
2 Related work54
Leaderboards and judging designs. Arena collects user preferences over anonymous model55
responses and estimates model scores and uncertainty [2]. MT-Bench supplies a curated question set56
and human and GPT-4 assessments, while documenting judge biases and consistency issues [6]. We57
reuse their published labels; no new model responses or paid judge calls are generated. Our paired58
analysis uses common question–turn–model-pair cells rather than assuming that the full human and59
GPT-4 releases have the same sampling pattern.60
Influence and finite-sample robustness. Koh and Liang [4] use inverse-Hessian derivatives to61
trace the effect of changing observation weights. Broderick et al. [1] distinguish exact maximum62
perturbations from first-order approximations, and stress that refitting a candidate deletion can63
demonstrate non-robustness but cannot certify robustness when the candidate fails. They also64
distinguish sign changes, significance changes, and statistically significant opposite conclusions. We65
retain these distinctions: our standardized effects are reference-scale quantities, not new significance66
tests.67
Ranking sensitivity and manipulation. Huang et al. [3] already apply AMIP to BT score dif-68
ferences and study deletion sensitivity on Arena and MT-Bench, including human versus model69
judgments. We credit that core application rather than presenting it as new. Min et al. [5] study70
practical vote rigging in Arena, including identification and attack feasibility. Our fixed-record71
outcome reversals assume knowledge and editing access that a real user may not have; we do not72
reproduce a live manipulation system. The narrower addition is a cross-domain audit of the edit73
denominator and strict endpoint constraints, paired with uncertainty and edit-unit provenance. This74
is not an exhaustive novelty claim: the task imposed a bounded literature-access protocol, and only75
explicitly permitted sources were consulted (Appendix H.1).76

3.4 Effect size and uncertainty124
The primary continuous endpoint at nominal 1% is the exact margin reduction m0 − mS. We also125
report D = (m0 − mS)/s0, where s0 is the SD of the original fixed-pair cluster-bootstrap margins.126
This standardizes a change against ordinary original-data variation; D is not a post-edit z-test. Pair127
reversal mS < 0 and incidental winner change are separate outcomes.128
Three different variations are kept apart. First, 300 original-data cluster-bootstrap replicates give129
s0 (sample SD, divisor 299) and percentile intervals. Second, 100 random-edit seeds per budget130
give empirical 2.5–97.5% subset ranges, and Wilson intervals for Monte Carlo reversal probabilities.131
These ranges do not estimate population sampling uncertainty. Third, a post-outcome exploratory132
extension repeats fitting, set selection, and refitting within 300 cluster-bootstrap samples at 1%; its133
percentile ranges describe the resampled procedure, not the effect of a fixed set. All percentages134
use integer floors. No hypothesis-testing sweep is performed, and none of the reported intervals is135
presented as simultaneous or selection-adjusted coverage.136

5 Results168
5.1 A global fraction can conceal concentrated exposure169

each of the three NBA seasons, but not in either full MT cohort (Table 2). In Arena, 574 reversals171
touch up to 17.41% of one model’s incident record. Strict caps permit 555 selected edits and reduce172
the standardized effect from 22.494 to 1.881. The two procedures use similar global counts but173
different exposure allocations. Capping still reverses Arena’s tiny original margin; the contrast is an174
effect-size difference, not a universal defense.175

5.2 Point flips, random noise, and original uncertainty differ187
Arena’s original margin is only 0.004, with percentile interval [−0.103, 0.106]. The original leader188
remains first in only 52% of original-data bootstrap replicates. Uniform 1% random reversals switch189
the pair in 35% of runs, while uncapped targeted reversals produce a much larger score change. A190
random or capped point flip therefore does not, by itself, destroy a well-established original separation.191
It can coexist with a valid warning that a sufficiently targeted edit changes scores substantially.192
In the other full cohorts, random 1% reversals occur in zero or one of 100 trials; zero observed193
trials is not a zero-probability conclusion. Targeted reductions are substantially larger than random194
mean reductions (Figure 1), including where they fail to reverse the pair. The NBA2015 unperturbed195
interval excludes zero under the game-bootstrap approximation, yet its uncapped 1% witness reverses196
the pair. Conversely, MT’s larger margins can survive an adversarial reduction of more than two197
original SDs. A flip count alone misses both distinctions.198

5.3 Matched judges: separation is not the same as response204
The matched panels have exactly the same cells and endpoint degrees. GPT-4’s original GPT-4-205
versus-Claude margin exceeds the human margin by 0.479, with paired question-bootstrap interval206
[0.173, 0.795] (Table 3). Both retain positive margins under the 1% global edit procedure, although207
the human panel is much closer to a reversal. Their standardized uncapped reductions, 2.211 and208
2.050, are similar in magnitude. This supports a distinction between initial separation and response209
to a particular perturbation, not a claim that a model judge is more accurate, less biased, or generally210
safer.211
5.4 Specification checks expose non-universality212
The main full-cohort reversal pattern persists under the two ridge alternatives, but this is not invariance213
to the entire modeling pipeline. Dropping exact ties already switches the original Arena top pairbefore214
editing; attributing that switch to the edit would be incorrect. Excluding inconsistent GPT-4 outcomes215
and restricting human judge sources change both initial margins and the effective denominators216
(Tables 9–10). Whole-question reversals produce another response curve because a question spans217
many correlated judgments, rather than being an interchangeable vote (Table 11).218
Static and batch-adaptive outcomes, cap rounding, and integer-linear selection are reported together in219
Table 8. Exact-refit checks are essential: the Arena 1% deletion control predicts a reduction of about220
0.764 but actually reduces the margin by about 2.281. This is finite-perturbation nonlinearity, despite221
accurate infinitesimal derivatives. Appendix E gives the complete linear-versus-refit diagnostic and222
grid witnesses without claiming optimal thresholds.223
6 Discussion and limitations224
Scope of the finding. The supported claim is not that leaderboards are universally fragile or robust.225
It is that a global fraction does not specify exposure, and small point-rank changes have different226
interpretations depending on initial separation and feasible edits. These statements follow from actual227
counterfactual refits and explicit denominators. The strict-cap comparison is an audit of a different228
admissible set, not evidence that platforms enforce such caps or that they remove all dangerous229
perturbations.230
Statistical limits. The top pair is selected from the same data and held fixed; intervals are descriptive,231
not winner-selection-adjusted inference. Prompt-string clustering does not recover user dependence,232
question clustering does not model shared judges across questions, and NBA game resampling does233
not capture serial, team, home-court, or season dynamics. Static BT may be misspecified, especially234
for changing sports strengths or nontransitive model preferences. A ridge-finite fit need not be a235
well-specified generative model. No cross-domain ordering should be interpreted causally, and six236
full cohorts do not estimate a population prevalence of vulnerable leaderboards.237
Perturbation and computational limits. We assume offline access to the observed schedule and238
outcomes. Real manipulation has costs, detection, and access restrictions absent here. Tie reversals,239
deletions, question edits, and averaged-cell reversals have different semantics. Same-λ sum-loss fits240
have different relative regularization across sample sizes. Static influence and its integer surrogate do241

6.1 Broader impact and research process246
Exposure-aware reporting can help readers avoid unsupported selection and marketing claims. Con-247
versely, edit-selection code could be repurposed to manipulate evaluations or to exaggerate the248
significance of a harmless near-tie. We release an offline audit with exposure and uncertainty diagnos-249
tics, no live-voting integration, no account identification, and no raw conversations in the code archive.250
These choices reduce operational and privacy risks but are not complete misuse safeguards. No new251
participants were recruited or identified; all analyses use public licensed historical records. No new252
institutional review was obtained, and no unverified approval or exemption is asserted. Substantial253
LLM assistance and the restricted-access protocol are disclosed in the appendix.254
7 Conclusion255
A useful stability report should state what is edited, whose evidence is affected, how many edits are256
actually feasible, and which conclusion changes relative to original uncertainty. On the tested snap-257
shots, global influence-selected reversals, strict exposure caps, and random errors lead to materially258
different interpretations. Reporting only a small global fraction or a flipped first-place label omits259
those distinctions. Our evidence is a set of reproducible conditional witnesses and controls, not a260
universal robustness verdict.261
