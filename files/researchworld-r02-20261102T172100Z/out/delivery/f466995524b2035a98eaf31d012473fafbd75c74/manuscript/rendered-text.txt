                                                         Abstract
1              Conformal regions for an observed composition require simultaneous-vector
2              coverage, subgroup diagnostics and a common measure of efficiency. We
3              compare split-conformal scores and calibration schemes in controlled logistic-
4              normal regression using 100 independent full-dataset repetitions per main cell,
5              three or ten components, and a shared fitted predictor. At three compo-
6              nents with heterogeneous noise and 499 calibration observations, global Aitchi-
7              son joint coverage is 0.9017[0.8988, 0.9045], but high-noise-group coverage is
8              only 0.5069[0.4931, 0.5207]. A shift increasing that group’s frequency reduces
9              marginal coverage to 0.6050[0.5944, 0.6157], while observed-group calibration
10             reaches 0.9055[0.9013, 0.9097]. Learned scaling improves adaptation but still
11             misses the group target in a ten-component case; correct oracle weighting re-
12             stores shifted marginal coverage without guaranteeing group coverage. Common
13             Euclidean simplex-area estimates reveal setting-dependent efficiency differences,
14             with coverage reported alongside size. Coordinatewise calibration can substan-
15             tially undercover the whole vector, and zero replacement requires distinguishing
16             direct raw-response membership from an explicit inverse-image region, which
17             inherits transformed coverage for original responses. The contribution is a re-
18             producible diagnostic evaluation under explicit synthetic mechanisms, not a new
19             coverage theorem or a universal method ranking.


20   1    Introduction
21   A predicted probability vector and an uncertain compositional response are different statistical ob-
22   jects. A classifier may output a vector of probabilities while its observed outcome is a single label.
23   Here the response itself is observed as a nonnegative vector summing to one. Examples include
24   measured proportions and repeated-measurement distributions. Our coverage event is simultane-
25   ous inclusion of this entire vector in a region of the simplex; covering a label or each coordinate
26   separately answers a different question.
27   Split conformal prediction provides a finite-sample marginal coverage guarantee under exchange-
28   ability, for a score fixed independently of calibration data. This leaves several choices open. Raw-
29   coordinate and log-ratio distances produce different regions. A global calibration threshold averages
30   over heterogeneous populations. Smaller regions can reflect undercoverage, and radii in different
31   geometries have incompatible units. Finally, a guarantee averaging over calibration samples need
32   not describe the performance of every realized calibration sample.
33   We study these choices in a controlled, synthetic compositional regression benchmark. The design
34   uses a shared fitted predictor, independent repetitions of the full training–calibration–test experi-
35   ment, and explicit subgroup and distribution-shift diagnostics. It separates four questions: whether
36   coordinatewise calibration delivers simultaneous coverage; whether marginal coverage conceals

38   conclusions survive a controlled mixture shift, finite calibration ranks, and zero replacement. The
39   contribution is an evaluation design and its reproducible evidence, rather than a new conformal guar-
40   antee. The controlled setting exposes mechanisms; it cannot establish performance for a particular
41   application or a universal ranking of methods.
42   Our theory-based expectations are that correctly ranked joint scores achieve iid marginal cover-
43   age, naive coordinate calibration can miss the joint target, and heterogeneity can hide group under-
44   coverage that becomes more consequential under mixture shift. We test whether scaling or group
45   calibration improves this behavior and changes common-measure area; no geometry is presumed
46   universally best. These hypotheses concern the controlled mechanisms, not all compositions.

47   2   Related work and positioning
48   Split conformal prediction separates fitting from calibration and gives marginal coverage under
49   exchangeability; its computational simplicity motivates holding the predictor fixed while varying
50   scores and calibration schemes [Lei et al., 2017, Angelopoulos and Bates, 2022]. Full conformal
51   and refitting-based alternatives make different computational and statistical tradeoffs. Jackknife+
52   uses leave-one-out fits and has a general worst-case 1 − 2α lower bound in its ordinary construction
53   [Barber et al., 2020]. We do not benchmark every conformal family or claim a new rank guarantee.
54   The closest permitted work is Javanmardi et al. [2024]. They already split-conformalize distribution-
55   vector labels into simplex regions, compare scores including total variation, KL divergence, Wasser-
56   stein distance and model-based alternatives, and evaluate coverage and relative simplex size across
57   repeated splits. Thus conformalizing a composition, varying distributional scores and measuring a
58   fraction of the simplex are established ideas. Their distinction between noisy observed distribution
59   labels and latent clean probabilities is also directly relevant: coverage for one does not automati-
60   cally establish coverage for the other. Our focus is a shared clr-regression predictor under controlled
61   heterogeneity, coordinate simultaneity, calibration variation, a known group-mixture intervention
62   and boundary sensitivity. The contribution must be assessed through those completed comparisons,
63   rather than through a claim that simplex regions are new.
64   Classification prediction sets instead cover labels, even when their scores use a softmax vector [An-
65   gelopoulos et al., 2022]. Class-conditional and clustered calibration illustrate how marginal guar-
66   antees can hide class failures and how fine strata increase calibration demands [Ding et al., 2023].
67   Our strata are observed features, not response classes. Multivalid conformal methods address richer
68   collections of groups and thresholds [Jung et al., 2022]; evaluating two groups or calibrating within
69   them does not establish such a general property.
70   Weighted conformal prediction handles specified covariate shift when the conditional response law
71   is invariant and appropriate likelihood ratios are known [Tibshirani et al., 2020]. The oracle base-
72   line here isolates the effect of exact ratios under a deliberately transparent shift. It is not evidence
73   that estimated weights work without additional assumptions. Finally, coverage parity does not itself
74   establish equitable downstream decisions: human-subject classification evidence makes this distinc-
75   tion concrete [Cresswell et al., 2025]. No human-utility or fairness conclusion follows from this
76   numerical benchmark.

119   4   Experimental design
120   Controlled response model. Features are X = (G, Z1 , Z2 , Z3 ), where G ∼ Bernoulli(0.2)
121   and the Zj are independent standard normals, independent of G.                          With ϕ(X) =
122   (1, G, Z1 , Z2 , Z3 , Z1 Z2 , Z12 ) and a fixed, dimension-specific coefficient matrix B, generate
                          Y = softmax{ϕ(X)B + σ(X)(a ⊙ E)},               E ∼ N (0, IK ).                   (3)
123   The positive axis weights a increase linearly from 0.65 to 1.35 before normalization to mean square
124   one. One coefficient matrix per dimension is fixed across replications; the study does not average
125   over coefficient draws. Homogeneous noise has σ(X) = 0.45. Heterogeneous noise has σ(X) =
126   0.18 exp(0.15Z1 ) for G = 0 and 0.95 exp(0.15Z1 ) for G = 1. This deliberately creates a known
127   high-noise minority group. All constants and coefficient seeds are fixed in the executable generator.
128   The predictor fits ridge regression of clr responses on ϕ, using 1,000 independent training obser-
129   vations and penalty 10−3 on every coefficient except the intercept. Its predicted composition is
130   the softmax of fitted logits. A second training-only ridge regression predicts log squared clr-residual
131   norm, whose clipped half-exponential gives the positive scale (Appendix B). The predictor estimates
132   a clr center; it is not generally the conditional arithmetic mean of Y . The correctly specified log-ratio
133   mean structure favors this modeling approach and limits extrapolation to misspecified predictors.

141   Design chronology. The seven core scores/calibration controls were fixed after an implementa-
142   tion/runtime pilot and before the main run. The known-ratio reference was proposed before con-
143   firmatory outcomes. The plan owner requested TV and forward-KL after the core job started for

148   Focused scope checks. A separate rank experiment varies calibration size and nominal error with
149   continuous and tied scores, including ranks that require an infinite threshold. A boundary sensitivity
150   censors small components to exact zeros and compares original-target raw calibration with consis-
151   tently transformed clr calibration on Tϵ (Y ). It uses 50 fresh repetitions per dimension, n = 499, the
152   heterogeneous mechanism and ϵ ∈ {10−8 , 10−4 , 10−2 }. The same original data are shared across
153   replacements. A separately labeled diagnostic applies transformed-calibrated clr regions to original
154   responses; it has no original-target nominal guarantee. An explicit inverse-image region instead in-
155   herits transformed coverage for original responses by exact event equivalence. Appendix B specifies
156   both membership conventions.

157   Uncertainty and reporting. The unit of uncertainty is an independently generated full dataset
158   repetition, not an individual test observation. We report pointwise 95% Student-t intervals for mean
159   metrics and paired differences between methods using the same repetition. These intervals include
160   training, calibration and finite-test variability; they are not simultaneous intervals over all com-
161   parisons. Test-estimated coverage distributions additionally describe realized-calibration variation,
162   without certifying population coverage for individual calibration sets. Efficiency estimates use sep-
163   arate feature anchors; candidate Monte Carlo precision is retained separately from across-repetition
164   variation. The main area estimate uses 32 new feature anchors and 8,192 candidates per anchor and
165   environment. Unresolved tiny areas do not establish zero volume or a reliable ranking.


166   5   Results

167   Unless specified otherwise, numerical examples below use the heterogeneous mechanism, n = 499,
168   and pointwise 95% intervals across independent full-dataset repetitions. They illustrate prespecified
169   comparisons; all dimensions, calibration sizes, scenarios and environments remain in the delivered
170   cell summaries. An interval near nominal is not an equivalence test or proof of exact validity.

171   Joint calibration is necessary for joint reliability. At K = 3, iid joint coverage is
172   0.8390[0.8357, 0.8424] for naive coordinate calibration, 0.9360[0.9339, 0.9380] for Bonferroni,
173   0.9016[0.8987, 0.9044] for raw L2 and 0.9017[0.8988, 0.9045] for global Aitchison. The naive
174   control falls to 0.6380[0.6328, 0.6432] at K = 10. Thus coordinatewise calibration can fail sub-
175   stantially at the vector level, despite each coordinate being calibrated separately. Bonferroni is a
176   valid but conservative control. Joint-score iid marginal means across the core matrix remain close to
177   their rank targets; this does not mean every individual pointwise interval contains 0.9 or that regions
178   have equal size.

252   6   Discussion and limitations
253   The evaluation separates the target, the validity assumptions and the efficiency measure. A joint
254   region must be judged by simultaneous vector coverage. Marginal validity must be accompanied
255   by subgroup diagnostics when subgroup reliability matters. An efficiency comparison needs both
256   coverage and a shared measure of size. These principles describe how to interpret the experiments;
257   the evidence supports only the specific models, distributions and interventions actually tested.

258   Method choice under the tested conditions. Use a joint score or an explicit multiplicity correc-
259   tion when the whole vector must be covered; naive coordinate calibration failed that target. For
260   predeclared observed groups requiring separate reliability, group calibration fits the tested within-
261   group-preserving shift, subject to sparse-group costs. Training-only scaling improves adaptation
262   and expected iid area here, but its measured high-noise-group miss at ten components rules out a
263   general group guarantee. Known-ratio weighting addresses shifted marginal coverage under its as-
264   sumptions, while the oracle group miss shows why the two goals remain distinct. For zeros, calibrate
265   a boundary-compatible score on the original response or declare a transformed/inverse-image target
266   explicitly. No single raw or log-ratio geometry is an application-independent efficiency winner.
267   The synthetic design offers known ground truth, controlled heterogeneity and repeatable indepen-
268   dent calibration experiments. It lacks a real application, an application-specific loss for region size,
269   and a diversity of fitted predictors. The generator’s log-ratio mean belongs to the fitted basis. Ob-
270   served groups are supplied without estimation error, and the shift preserves their conditional laws
271   exactly. Unknown groups, misspecified density ratios, changes within groups and deployment-time
272   dependence can invalidate the corresponding interpretation. Neither a learned scale nor good empir-
273   ical subgroup coverage establishes distribution-free pointwise conditional coverage.
274   Euclidean simplex area is only one reference measure. It emphasizes different parts of the simplex
275   from a log-ratio measure and is evaluated only at K = 3. Results at K = 10 concern coverage and
276   declared within-geometry diagnostics. We do not infer a high-dimensional efficiency ranking from
277   low-dimensional area. Pointwise intervals across many cells do not provide multiplicity-controlled
278   discoveries or prove exact validity from finite simulation.
279   Ordinary clr is undefined at zeros. A positive replacement applied consistently to all splits pro-
280   duces a different, exchangeable response Tϵ (Y ); its coverage does not guarantee direct mem-
281   bership of the original response in the transformed clr region. An explicit inverse-image region
282   {y : Tϵ (y) ∈ Cϵ (x)} does inherit the same coverage for original compositions, including zeros, by
283   exact event identity. Its original-simplex area was not evaluated. Censoring in the sensitivity study is
284   a measurement mechanism, not a model for every kind of structural zero. Choosing a scientifically
285   justified zero mechanism remains application dependent.
286   We compare split-conformal score and calibration choices with one shared predictor. Full confor-
287   mal, jackknife+, learned density regions and other multivariate methods may trade computation,
288   adaptation and efficiency differently. Their absence restricts the comparison; it does not imply infe-
289   riority. No novelty claim rests on exhaustive discovery of the literature, and no conclusion is claimed
290   for latent class probabilities inferred from one observed label.

291   Potential impacts. Better diagnostics can prevent users from treating an average coverage guar-
292   antee as assurance for an undercovered population. Conversely, presenting a compact region or a
293   nominal guarantee without its target, group and shift conditions can encourage unwarranted con-
294   fidence in consequential decisions. This synthetic study neither measures downstream harm nor
295   certifies equitable decisions. Deployment would require relevant data, subgroup definitions, scien-
296   tific treatment of zeros and monitoring for changes beyond the tested mixture intervention.
