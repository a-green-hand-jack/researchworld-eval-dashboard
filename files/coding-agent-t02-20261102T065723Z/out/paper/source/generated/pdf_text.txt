WIDTH AND DELAYED GENERALIZATION
IN SMALL MODULAR NETWORKS
Anonymous authors
Paper under double-blind review
ABSTRACT
Model size can affect both fitting and the discovery of algorithmic structure, making
its relation to delayed generalization ambiguous. We test a strong decay-clock
explanation: widening chiefly accelerates fitting, while weight decay sets a largely
width-independent generalization time. We run 255 non-pilot experiments on
small modular networks, with five paired seeds per grid/control cell and a separate
ten-pair replication. At the reference setting, widths 64 and 256 both interpolate,
but only the wider models reach sustained 95% held-out accuracy within the
horizon; their restricted mean waiting-time difference is -18,220 steps (95% paired
bootstrap interval [-19,020, -17,180]). The new-seed replication gives 0/10 versus
10/10 generalization events. Matching expected initial norm preserves the contrast.
Fixing hidden directions after 1,000 updates preserves training interpolation but
prevents all five wide-model generalization events, whereas fixing them after 10,000
updates retains three. An explicit 61-unit ReLU circuit represents the target exactly,
separating expressivity from training outcomes. These observations contradict the
strong width-independent clock and support a role for continued feature learning.
Width effects depend on regularization, activation, and data coverage; censored runs
do not establish permanent failure, and no unique circuit mechanism is identified.
1
INTRODUCTION
A network can achieve almost perfect training accuracy on a small arithmetic table and only much
later predict unseen entries correctly. This delayed generalization, or grokking, makes a seemingly
elementary model-selection question surprisingly subtle: should a researcher use a smaller or a larger
model to observe it? Increasing capacity might accelerate memorization and widen the delay, improve
the discovery of reusable features and shorten it, or enable generalization that a narrower network
never reaches within the budget.
These possibilities require measuring more than final accuracy or the gap between successful runs.
If a narrow model memorizes but fails to generalize before a deadline, discarding it can reverse an
apparent width trend. Likewise, a large model that generalizes early and a small model that cannot
even fit are different failure modes for an experiment intended to exhibit grokking.
We independently construct a CPU study of modular addition with one hidden layer. Our initial
explanation is deliberately strong and falsifiable: width mainly changes the fitting time, whereas
regularization supplies the slower generalization clock. Fresh-seed experiments test this explanation
rather than selecting only trajectories that display it. An alternative, suggested during a separate
pilot, is a conditional feature-discovery account: width changes the availability or optimization
accessibility of structured solutions even after interpolation is possible, and regularization and data
coverage determine which solutions are selected. This is an operational explanation, not a claim to
identify a unique circuit.
Our contributions are a fully crossed width–decay comparison with explicit finite-horizon censoring;
paired-seed uncertainty for waiting-time contrasts; and controls that probe whether a width effect
survives initial norm matching and changes with the task or representation. All empirical figures and
tables are generated from saved runs. The intended contribution is a bounded, reproducible empirical
test; we do not claim a first analysis of model capacity or a complete literature survey.

RELATED WORK
Power et al. (2022) introduced grokking in small algorithmically generated datasets and emphasized
strong dependence on data size and regularization. Our study uses the same table-completion principle
but varies hidden width in a simpler network, distinguishing fitting, delayed generalization, and
right-censored runs.
Nanda et al. (2023) reverse engineered Fourier-based modular-addition circuits and separated memo-
rization, circuit formation, and cleanup. Inspired by the need for a continuous structural diagnostic, we
measure output equivariance to additive shifts. This is a task-specific projection, not a reconstruction
of their Transformer circuits or evidence that all our networks implement the same mechanism.
Liu et al. (2023) explained grokking through a mismatch between training and test loss landscapes as
a function of weight norm. Varma et al. (2023) proposed competition between circuits with different
norm efficiency and learning speeds. These accounts motivate our decay and norm controls. A failure
of a simple decay clock would constrain a narrow interpretation of these ideas, not refute the broader
theories.
Kumar et al. (2024) showed that delayed feature learning can produce grokking through a transition
from lazy to rich dynamics, including settings without explicit regularization. We do not measure
the neural tangent kernel or infer such a transition from accuracy alone. Davies et al. (2023)
connected grokking and double descent through pattern learning speeds, including a capacity-
dependent demonstration. Consequently, our novelty lies in the particular factorial test, censoring-
aware comparison, and controls, rather than the observation that capacity can affect generalization.
We use decoupled AdamW regularization as defined by Loshchilov & Hutter (2019).

EXPLANATIONS AND FALSIFIABLE PREDICTIONS
Let Tfit and Tgen denote sustained training and held-out accuracy events. The strong decay-clock
explanation says that larger width principally provides a faster route to memorization; the later
transition is governed by the decay of a poorly generalizing component. A component changed only
by AdamW shrinkage obeys

For small ηλ, its time scale is approximately (ηλ)−1. Equation 1 is an exact statement about pure
shrinkage, not a model of the complete gradient dynamics. Applying it to grokking adds substantive
assumptions about the memorizing component, its threshold, and ongoing feature learning.
We test three implications of the strong account. First, at fixed decay, increasing width should not
accelerate generalization substantially more than it accelerates interpolation; otherwise width acts on
the slow process as well. The primary comparison is width 64 versus 256 at λ = 1. Second, when
generalization occurs, ηλTgen should approximately collapse across positive decays; a factor of two
is our prechosen descriptive tolerance. Third, if width acts largely through its growing initial norm,
matching that norm should substantially remove the width effect. These predictions are operational
tests of the strong account; failure of one suffices to reject it in this setting.
The conditional feature-discovery account instead predicts (i) different generalization outcomes at
widths that both interpolate; (ii) a width–decay interaction rather than a single width curve; and (iii)
persistence of a width difference after expected initial norm matching. Rising additive-symmetry
energy would be consistent with this account, but is observational evidence. We treat it as an
explanation supported or challenged by interventions, not a proved necessary mechanism. The pilot
informed this alternative and the setting selection; all formal seeds are distinct from the pilot seed.

Expressivity check.
A constructive oracle ReLU classifier with 2p −1 hidden units computes
addition exactly (Appendix C); an eight-unit square network also suffices. Both are evaluated
exhaustively by the included code. Thus at p = 31, the reference width-64 ReLU class contains a
correct solution: its failure to generalize within the horizon is not proof of inadequate expressive
capacity. The constructions encode the known formula and are not learned baselines; they need not
have the same norm efficiency as the solutions preferred by training.
Optimization and design.
Every run performs 30,000 full-batch cross-entropy updates with
AdamW: learning rate 0.003, β = (0.9, 0.999), ϵ = 10−8, and constant decay applied to both
matrices. There is no scheduler, dropout, minibatching, checkpoint selection, or early stopping.
The main grid crosses all five widths with λ ∈{0, 0.3, 1, 3} and seeds 10, . . . , 14, giving 100 runs.
Dataset partitions are paired across widths and decay within each seed; independent seed streams
generate partitions, initialization, and random labels. Pairing controls the sampled partition but
does not make differently shaped initial matrices identical. Each process uses one CPU thread.
Reference/control batches use four workers; two-worker exploratory or replication batches can
overlap, with at most six training processes sharing a four-core CPU quota.
Events, censoring, and delay.
We evaluate at step zero and every 100 updates. An event requires
three consecutive checkpoints at or above 99% training accuracy or 95% held-out accuracy; its
time is the first checkpoint of the qualifying triple. This yields an effective ascertainment horizon
H = 29,800, because events beginning after H cannot be confirmed by step 30,000. A qualifying
event need not imply permanent stability; a retrospective sensitivity analysis additionally requires all
remaining observed checkpoints to stay above threshold. A missing event is right censored at H and is
never described as proof of permanent failure. We call a confirmed event delayed if Tgen ≥2Tfit and
held-out accuracy at Tfit is at most 50%. This definition excludes trajectories that already generalize
when fitting occurs; it is an operational criterion, not a universal definition of grokking. Thresholds
of 90% and 99% are a sensitivity analysis.
Independent replication.
After completing the main grid, we specified a separate replication cohort
on seeds 20–29 for widths 64 and 256 at the reference setting (twenty runs). It was motivated by the
limited inferential resolution of five pairs and is reported separately rather than pooled retrospectively
into the primary comparison. Its paired bootstrap uses 100,000 resamples with analysis RNG seed
20261102, and both cohorts report an exact two-sided sign test.
The primary endpoint is the restricted waiting time τ = min(Tgen, H). Since censoring is adminis-
trative and shared, its mean is directly observable without a parametric survival model. A mean of H
means that no qualifying event was observed; it is not an estimated event time. We compare width
256 minus width 64 within seed. Percentile 95% bootstrap intervals enumerate all 55 resamples of
the five seed pairs for the original grid. Tables also report final accuracy. For event proportions, exact
binomial intervals are wide: even 5/5 has a 95% interval of approximately [0.478, 1], and 0/5 has
[0, 0.522]. Seed variability includes split variability, not sampling uncertainty over a fixed held-out
table. Secondary comparisons are descriptive, without a multiple-testing claim.

256 (Figure 3). This diagnostic is consistent with a change in output structure when it accompanies
better accuracy, but its definition already uses the task symmetry and it cannot independently establish
why a feature formed.
The exploratory direction-freezing intervention after 1,000 steps gives 0/5 versus 0/5 generalization
events at widths 64 and 256. Mean final training accuracies are 1.000 and 1.000, and final held-out
accuracies are 0.007 and 0.022, respectively. The width-256 trajectories are shown in Appendix
Figure 4.
The exploratory direction-freezing intervention after 10,000 steps gives 0/5 versus 3/5 generalization
events at widths 64 and 256. Mean final training accuracies are 1.000 and 1.000, and final held-out
accuracies are 0.523 and 0.960, respectively. The width-256 trajectories are shown in Appendix
Figure 4.
A step advantage need not be an advantage at a common compute proxy. For a budget of 64 × 30,000
hidden-unit updates, the actually evaluated checkpoints are width 64 at 30,000 steps, width 128 at
15,000 steps, width 256 at 7,500 steps. Their mean held-out accuracies are 0.716, 0.928, 0.830,
respectively. This proxy reflects the width-dependent dense-layer arithmetic at fixed data size; it
excludes fixed framework and optimizer overhead and is not measured wall-clock equality. Full
intervals and per-seed values are supplied in the appendix.

Persistence of the contrast under expected initial norm matching argues against initial total norm

circuit nor a general necessity theorem.
The operational distinction between occurrence and latency matters. A configuration with no
confirmed event cannot be assigned a small delay; a configuration with both rapid fitting and later
generalization can exhibit a large ratio even when its absolute waiting time decreases. The success
map, restricted waiting time, and delayed-event counts therefore answer complementary questions.
Finite-horizon success is an experimentally useful setting-selection criterion, but it is not a universal
phase boundary.
This study varies hidden width in a shallow, bias-free architecture with symbolic one-hot inputs and
full-batch AdamW. It does not establish a scaling law for depth, Transformers, language models, other
optimizers, or natural data. Standard initialization and a fixed learning rate define the experimental
intervention; changing parameterization or matching update sizes might change the relation. Matching
expected total norm addresses one specific confound, but does not hold feature distributions, logits,
or gradient dynamics fixed.
Finite-horizon non-generalization is a central limitation. Extending the runs could convert censored
observations into delayed events and change their event-time ordering. The observed contrast in
restricted waiting time remains valid for this horizon; it cannot establish that a width is incapable
of eventual generalization. Five seeds permit a paired comparison but cannot tightly estimate
event probabilities or rare outcomes. Bootstrap intervals from five observations are descriptive
and may under-cover; a degenerate interval for all censored seeds conveys no uncertainty about
performance beyond H. Because the whole finite operation table is evaluated, uncertainty comes from
training randomness and splits, not from pretending that table entries are independent experimental
replications.
The setting and alternative explanation were informed by seed-zero pilot experiments. The new-seed
grid and primary contrast were fixed before those formal runs, but this is not a publicly preregistered
study. The secondary controls and threshold analyses are multiple exploratory comparisons. The
structural diagnostic is specific to addition and uses known task symmetry; it supports a statement
about output organization, not a unique explanation of optimization. An exhaustive novelty assessment
is also unavailable: literature access was deliberately restricted to the supplied starter papers to respect
the independence requirement. Claims are accordingly scoped to this experiment.

CONCLUSION
In this finite-horizon modular study, increasing width can enable delayed generalization and markedly
accelerate the slow process after training interpolation is already possible. This rejects our strong
width-independent decay-clock conjecture. The width relation changes with regularization and the
task controls, and initial expected norm alone is insufficient wherever the matched-norm contrast
persists. The supported explanation is conditional access to structured solutions under the complete
training setup, with the particular circuit mechanism and eventual outcomes beyond the horizon still
unresolved.

C
CONSTRUCTIVE EXPRESSIVITY WITNESSES
Let s = a + b ∈{0, . . . , 2p −2}. A bias-free first-layer row with operand coefficients a −k/2 and
b −k/2 produces s −k, because each operand is one hot. Use hidden features hk(s) = (s −k)+ for
k = −1, . . . , 2p −3, a total of 2p −1 units. Features with k ≥2p −2 are identically zero over the
input domain and need not be stored. For integer j ≥0, set
qj(s) = hj−1(s) −2hj(s) + hj+1(s),
zc(s) = qc(s) + qc+p(s).
At integer s in the domain, qj(s) = 1{s = j}; consequently zc(s) = 1{s mod p = c}. This
constructs a correct classifier within the width-64 ReLU family for p = 31 by padding its 61 active
units with zeros. It is an upper bound on required expressive width, not a lower bound or an
optimization guarantee. In particular, its parameter norm can differ greatly from learned solutions.
For the square network, write Ca = cos(2πa/p) and Sa = sin(2πa/p). The eight squared linear
features are (Ca±Cb)2, (Sa±Sb)2, (Sa±Cb)2, and (Ca±Sb)2. Taking pairwise differences divided
by four recovers products; their linear combinations give cos(2π(a + b)/p) and sin(2π(a + b)/p).
A class-dependent linear readout yields cos(2π(a + b −c)/p), uniquely maximized at the correct
residue. The oracle uses no training data, but explicitly uses the arithmetic target.
src/construct circuit.py constructs both classifiers in float32 and verifies every or-
dered pair for both primes.
Its measured numerical identities and accuracies are archived in
generated/expressivity.json; it is separate from the training-run count.
