# Review 1 of the Brief (rev 1, 2026-11-18): the price of first place

Verdict: a good idea with a defensible contribution (a cardinal, capped bribery problem solved exactly and calibrated on real boards, with rule-by-rule comparison). The refutation conditions are a real strength. The main risks are (a) scope that will not fit 9 pages, (b) a cost scale and cap that decide the answer and are not yet pinned down, (c) one theory claim that is not true as stated, (d) a thesis that is partly tautological unless m is varied. Items are ordered by how much they change the paper.

## Strong (keep)
- The unit of analysis, the breadth-depth frontier C_A(k), is a real object that distinguishes rules; "no rule is uniformly cheapest, ranking of rules depends on the target" is falsifiable and the Brief states what would refute it (section 3 last paragraph). Keep these up front in the paper.
- Targeting multiplier (E4) answers the objective's "how much investment" in a way readers can use: targeted vs genuine uniform improvement.
- Three (N, m) regimes, official rule always included, solver checked against brute force, uncertainty planned (E5). This meets the venue's rigor criteria.
- The no-training, capped-boost framing is honest about what the data can say.

## Weak / must fix

1. **The cost scale decides the cross-rule comparison; fix it before any result.** The price is in points (or points/sigma_j). sigma_j is a property of the model population, so it changes with slice and with who is on the board; raw points ignore that tasks have different floors (OLLB normalises scores from chance) and headroom. Pick one primary unit, argue it, and show the headline ordering of rules survives the others (raw normalised points; sigma units; fraction of headroom to the cap). Without this, "rule A is cheaper than B" can be an artefact of units. Also state per rule how "price" relates to the aggregate scale: it is input-space cost, so comparable across rules; say so once.

2. **The cap C1 (frontier on every task) is a very strong, unrealistic ceiling and makes "feasible" nearly trivial.** A target raised to the per-task maximum on all tasks is a pointwise maximum of all models: it is weakly first under every monotone rule, so infeasibility under C1 arises only from ties (and non-monotone/rank-pairwise corner cases). Say this, and define "first" with a margin or tie-break so infeasibility is not just ties. More important, an unbounded-cap cost is not what a developer faces. Make the price a function of a *budget on boost size*: report the cheapest first place as a curve (price vs allowed per-task boost B, e.g. B in {2,5,10,20 pts} or in sigma units) and the minimum B at which first place becomes feasible under each rule. That is a more informative object than one price per cap and is robust to the C1/C2/C3 choice.

3. **Calibrate "what a targeted boost looks like" from the data, not only from caps.** OLLB `contents` has `Type`, `Merged`, `Base Model`, `#Params (B)` and `Submission Date` (checked on the frozen snapshot). Pairs (fine-tune, its base model) give an empirical distribution of per-task gains and of cross-task spillover from actual targeted training, on the same six tasks. Use it to (i) set a realistic boost scale B, (ii) estimate the spillover matrix instead of assuming it, (iii) say how often a required boost is inside the observed range. This turns "an assumption, flagged" into a measured sensitivity and answers the objective's request to justify the modelling assumptions. Check coverage of `Base Model` (non-null is not the same as a resolvable parent) before relying on it.

4. **Theory claim 3 is wrong as written.** Mean rank and mean win rate are additive over tasks, so the exact problem is a multiple-choice knapsack (DP is right). Plurality of task wins, Copeland and Schulze/ranked pairs are not additive: rival r is beaten only if the number of tasks where the target passes r exceeds a threshold, so choices couple across tasks (set-cover-like; bribery for such rules is NP-hard in the discrete setting). Split the claim: exact DP for additive-rank rules; MILP/enumeration for coupled rules with a reported optimality gap; for m = 6 enumeration of 2^6 task subsets with per-subset breakpoint search is exact. State the solver's size limit for N = 4576. Drop Schulze/ranked pairs: the Brief already allows this ("if affordable"); I recommend dropping them and Elo/BT from the main text so the paper holds ~7 rules.

5. **Claim 2 for the mean: breadth is not a price in raw linear cost.** With linear raw cost, the depth needed is the same however it is spread (the gap times m); only the minimal breadth changes, and the frontier is flat then infeasible. The frontier's non-flat shape appears only with sigma/convex costs. Say which cost the "mean is flat" statement uses, and avoid the impression that depth is cheaper under mean for reasons other than the ceiling/headroom limit.

6. **The thesis is partly a restatement of the rules' definitions unless m is varied.** "Median needs a majority of tasks" is known; with m = 6 (OLLB contents) the breadth difference between median (3) and mean (often 2-3, given ceilings) is small. What would be new and is cheap: **vary m on the same models.** OLLB `results` gives the same models at granularity m = 6 (benchmark level) and m ~ 40-50 (sub-tasks: BBH, MMLU-Pro subjects, GPQA, MuSR, MATH), and HELM MMLU gives m = 57 subjects, from which random task subsets of size m can be drawn. Plot price (breadth k*, depth) against m for each rule, with the benchmark-lottery-style bootstrap over subsets. This tests how the rules differ as a function of m, isolates N from m (the Brief currently confounds them across the three boards), and shows that task granularity is itself a design variable (investing in a sub-task vs a benchmark). I recommend making this a headline experiment.

7. **Rules differ in who the incumbent is, which contaminates the price comparison.** A target's price under rule A and under B is the cost of beating different leaders. Report a decomposition or a control: price under each rule against a *common* fixed incumbent set (the top-K under the official rule), alongside the rule-specific leader. Otherwise "A is cheaper than B" can mean only "A's leader is weaker".

8. **Crowded, near-duplicate top of OLLB.** The top of `contents` is a family of 72B-78B Qwen2.5 derivatives and merges (715 `Merged`, 1724 "base merges", 1683 not on the hub). Rank rules and "first with confidence" behave very differently when the top-10 are one model family within noise, and the price for a target ranked 4000 is not a meaningful developer scenario. Fix: define the target population up front (top-K by official rule, K ~ 20-50, plus a stratified sample for E1), define the incumbent and rival set (exclude merges / unavailable / flagged as a main slice, all as a sensitivity), and size-matched cap C2 is a main-text item, not a variant (a 7B model is not compared against 78B frontiers).

9. **"First with confidence" (E5) needs a definition.** Solving min cost s.t. P(first) >= 0.95 under score noise is a stochastic program. A tractable version: require a margin of c standard errors of the aggregate-difference against the incumbent (a margin epsilon in the deterministic solver), then validate P(first) by Monte Carlo on the optimal plan. HELM subjects have few items, so noise is large; OLLB `results` has stderr; the `contents` board has none, so say which board gets which noise model, and treat rank-rule noise by resampling.

10. **Make claims about "which rule is cheapest" testable.** Predefine: paired comparison across targets (fraction of targets where A < B with CI), rank agreement (Kendall tau of rule orderings across targets), and a regression of log price on gap, dispersion and correlation (E3) with held-out boards. Pre-register the refutation thresholds as numbers in the Brief now, not after seeing results.

## Prior work and novelty
- The novelty frame (cardinal bribery with ceilings, calibrated on real boards, rule-by-rule) is plausible against what the Brief lists; the starter list's Chen et al. (equilibrium, latent quality, no real-data rule comparison) and Zhang & Hardt (stability/diversity) are the closest. I did **not** search for further work on this question; I recommend you do not either (objective forbids it), and in particular **no forward-citation ("cited by") lookups on Zhang & Hardt, Chen et al. or any other starter-list paper**, since such pages may discuss the forbidden paper. If contact happens, stop reading and record it.
- Read Zhang & Hardt and Chen et al. in full before the related-work claims about their limitations enter the paper (the Brief already says so); until then, phrase the differences as "as we understand their abstract".
- Margin-of-victory / shift-bribery sources, Hampel/Huber for breakdown point and Blum & Hardt (Ladder) are still owed; verify or drop.

## Scope advice (stronger, not narrower)
Keep the full question; cut breadth of machinery, not depth of evidence. Suggested main text: (rules) mean, z-mean, geometric/p-mean as one family, median/quantile, min, mean rank, win rate, plurality, Copeland; (theory) 3 short propositions with proofs in the appendix (quantile, mean/p-mean, additive-rank DP) + one no-dominance instance; (experiments) price-vs-budget curves (item 2), breadth-depth frontiers, m-variation (item 6), targeting multiplier, uncertainty, cap/cost/tie sensitivity. Everything else to the appendix. Reproducibility: the whole pipeline is a few CPU hours, so run all targets, not a sample, for the small boards.

## What I will check on re-review
That the cost unit and the cap/budget are fixed and argued; theory claim 3 is split; the m-variation and base-model calibration are in or explicitly excluded with a reason; the target population and incumbent control are defined; the refutation thresholds are numeric.
